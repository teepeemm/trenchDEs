\section{Introduction to Systems of Differential Equations}
\label{sec:systems_intro}

Many physical situations are modeled by systems of $n$ differential
equations
in $n$ unknown functions, where $n\ge2$. The next three examples
illustrate physical problems that lead to systems of differential
equations. In these examples and throughout this chapter we'll
denote the independent variable by $t$.

\begin{example}\label{example:10.1.1}
Tanks $T_1$ and $T_2$ contain 100 gallons and 300 gallons of salt
solutions, respectively. Salt solutions are simultaneously added to
both tanks from external sources, pumped from each tank to the other,
and drained from both tanks (\zcref{figure:10.1.1}). A solution with
$1$ pound of salt per gallon is pumped into $T_1$ from an external source
at \qty{5}{\gallon\per\minute}, and a solution with $2$ pounds of salt per gallon is
pumped into $T_2$ from an external source at \qty{4}{\gallon\per\minute}. The solution
from $T_1$ is pumped into $T_2$ at \qty{2}{\gallon\per\minute}, and the solution from
$T_2$ is pumped into $T_1$ at \qty{3}{\gallon\per\minute}. $T_1$ is drained at \qty{6}{\gallon\per\minute}
and $T_2$ is drained at \qty{3}{\gallon\per\minute}. Let $Q_1(t)$ and $Q_2(t)$ be the
number of pounds of salt in $T_1$ and $T_2$, respectively, at time
$t>0$. Derive a system of differential equations for $Q_1$ and $Q_2$.
Assume that both mixtures are well stirred.
\end{example}

\begin{figure}
  \centering\color{blue}
  \includegraphics[alt={The tanks as described in the example},
  width=5.67in,height=3.3in,keepaspectratio]{fig100101}
  \caption{The tanks described in \zcref{example:10.1.1}.}
  \label{figure:10.1.1}
\end{figure}


\solution
As in \zcref{sec:cooling_mixing}, let \emph{\color{blue}rate in} and
\emph{\color{blue}rate out} denote the rates (\unit{\pound\per\minute}) at which salt enters and
leaves a tank; thus,
\begin{align*}
Q_1'&=(\text{rate in})_1-(\text{rate out})_1,\\
Q_2'&=(\text{rate in})_2-(\text{rate out})_2.
\end{align*}
Note that the volumes of the solutions in $T_1$ and $T_2$
remain constant at 100~gallons and 300~gallons, respectively.

$T_1$ receives salt from the external source at the rate of
\[
\qty{1}{\pound\per\gallon}\times\qty{5}{\gallon\per\minute}
=\qty{5}{\pound\per\minute},
\]
and from $T_2$ at the rate of
\[
(\unit{\pound\per\gallon}\text{ in }T_2)\times\qty{3}{\gallon\per\minute}
=\frac{1}{300}Q_2\times3=\frac{1}{100}Q_2 \unit{\pound\per\minute}.
\]
Therefore
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.1.1}
\textrm{(rate in)}_1= 5+\frac{1}{100}Q_2.
\end{equation}
Solution leaves $T_1$  at the rate of \qty{8}{\gallon\per\minute}, since \qty{6}{\gallon\per\minute} are
drained and \qty{2}{\gallon\per\minute} are pumped to $T_2$; hence,
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.1.2}
(\textrm{rate out})_1
=(\unit{\pound\per\gallon}\text{ in }T_1)\times \qty{8}{\gallon\per\minute}
=\frac{1}{100}Q_1\times8=\frac{2}{25}Q_1.
\end{equation}
\zcref{eq:10.1.1,eq:10.1.2}  imply that
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.1.3}
Q_1'=5+\frac{1}{100}Q_2-\frac{2}{25}Q_1.
\end{equation}

$T_2$ receives salt from the external source at the rate of
\[
\qty{2}{\pound\per\gallon}\times\qty{4}{\gallon\per\minute}
=\qty{8}{\pound\per\minute},
\]
and from $T_1$ at the rate of
\[
(\unit{\pound\per\gallon}\textrm{ in }T_1)\times\qty{2}{\gallon\per\minute}
=\frac{1}{100}Q_1\times2=\frac{1}{50}Q_1 \unit{\pound\per\minute}.
\]
Therefore
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.1.4}
\textrm{(rate in)}_2= 8+\frac{1}{50}Q_1.
\end{equation}
Solution leaves $T_2$  at the rate of \qty{6}{\gallon\per\minute},
since \qty{3}{\gallon\per\minute} are
drained and \qty{3}{\gallon\per\minute} are pumped to $T_1$; hence,
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.1.5}
(\textrm{rate out})_2
=(\unit{\pound\per\gallon}\textrm{ in }T_2)\times\qty{6}{\gallon\per\minute}
=\frac{1}{300}Q_2\times6=\frac{1}{50}Q_2.
\end{equation}
\zcref{eq:10.1.4,eq:10.1.5} imply that
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.1.6}
Q_2'=8+\frac{1}{50}Q_1-\frac{1}{50}Q_2.
\end{equation}

We say that \eqref{eq:10.1.3} and \eqref{eq:10.1.6}  form a \emph{\color{blue}system of two
first order equations in two unknowns}, and write them together as
\begin{align*}
Q_1'&=5-\frac{2}{25}Q_1+\frac{1}{100}Q_2\\
Q_2'&=8+\frac{1}{50}Q_1-\frac{1}{50}Q_2. \quad\bbox
\end{align*}


\begin{example}\label{example:10.1.2}
A mass $m_1$ is suspended from a rigid support on a spring $S_1$ and a
second mass $m_2$ is suspended from the first on a spring $S_2$
(\zcref{figure:10.1.2}). The springs obey Hooke's law, with spring
constants $k_1$ and $k_2$. Internal friction causes the springs to
exert damping forces proportional to the rates of change of their
lengths, with damping constants $c_1$ and $c_2$. Let $y_1=y_1(t)$ and
$y_2=y_2(t)$ be the displacements of the two masses from their
equilibrium positions at time $t$, measured positive upward.
 Derive a system of differential equations
for $y_1$ and $y_2$, assuming that the masses of the springs are
negligible and that vertical external forces $F_1$ and $F_2$ also act
on the objects.
\end{example}

\solution
In equilibrium, $S_1$ supports both $m_1$ and $m_2$ and $S_2$ supports
only $m_2$. Therefore, if $\Delta\ell_1$ and $\Delta\ell_2$ are the
elongations of the springs in equilibrium then
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.1.7}
(m_1+m_2)g=k_1\Delta\ell_1\quad\text{and}\quad m_2g=k_2\Delta\ell_2.
\end{equation}

    \draw[spring] (0, 4.5) -- (0, 1.5) node[midway, left=0.2cm] {Spring $S_2$};
    \node[mass] (m1) at (0, 5) {Mass $m_1$};
    \node[mass] (m2) at (0, 1) {Mass $m_2$};
    % Coordinates and Arrows
    \draw[dotted] (m1.south) -- (1.5, 4.5);

    \draw[dotted] (m2.south) -- (1.5, 0.5);
    \draw[arrow] (1.5, 0.5) -- (1.5, 2) node[above] {$y_2$};
\end{tikzpicture}
%  \includegraphics[alt={The spring system as described in the example.},
%  width=5.67in,height=3.3in,keepaspectratio]{fig100102}
  \caption{The spring system described in \zcref{example:10.1.2}.}
  \label{figure:10.1.2}
\end{figure}

Let $H_1$ be the Hooke's law force acting on $m_1$, and let $D_1$ be
the damping force on $m_1$. Similarly, let $H_2$ and $D_2$ be the
Hooke's law and damping forces acting on $m_2$. According to Newton's
second law of motion,
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.1.8}
\begin{aligned}
m_1y_1''&=-m_1g+H_1+D_1+F_1,\\
m_2y_2''&=-m_2g+H_2+D_2+F_2.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
When the displacements are $y_1$ and $y_2$, the change in length of
$S_1$ is $-y_1+\Delta\ell_1$ and the change in length of $S_2$ is
$-y_2+y_1+\Delta\ell_2$. Both springs exert Hooke's law forces on $m_1$,
while only $S_2$ exerts a Hooke's law force on $m_2$. These forces are
in directions that tend to restore the springs to their natural
lengths. Therefore
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.1.9}
H_1=k_1(-y_1+\Delta\ell_1)-k_2(-y_2+y_1+\Delta\ell_2)\quad\text{and}\quad
H_2=k_2(-y_2+y_1+\Delta\ell_2).
\end{equation}
When the velocities are $y_1'$ and $y_2'$,  $S_1$ and $S_2$ are
changing length at the rates $-y_1'$ and $-y_2'+y_1'$, respectively.
Both springs exert damping forces on $m_1$, while only $S_2$ exerts a
damping force on $m_2$. Since the force due to damping exerted by a
spring is proportional to the rate of change of length of the spring
and in a direction that opposes the change, it follows that
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.1.10}
D_1=-c_1y_1'+c_2(y_2'-y_1')\quad\text{and}\quad D_2=-c_2(y_2'-y_1').
\end{equation}

From \eqref{eq:10.1.8}, \eqref{eq:10.1.9}, and \eqref{eq:10.1.10},
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.1.11}
\begin{aligned}
m_1y_1''&=-m_1g+k_1(-y_1+\Delta\ell_1)-k_2(-y_2+y_1+\Delta\ell_2)
-c_1y_1'+c_2(y_2'-y_1')+F_1\\
&=-(m_1g-k_1\Delta\ell_1+k_2\Delta\ell_2)-k_1y_1+k_2(y_2-y_1)
-c_1y_1'+c_2(y_2'-y_1')+F_1
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.1.12}
\begin{aligned}
m_2y_2''
&=-m_2g+k_2(-y_2+y_1+\Delta\ell_2)-c_2(y_2'-y_1')+F_2\\
&=-(m_2g-k_2\Delta\ell_2)-k_2(y_2-y_1)-c_2(y_2'-y_1')+F_2.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
From \eqref{eq:10.1.7},
\[
m_1g-k_1\Delta\ell_1+k_2\Delta\ell_2=-m_2g+k_2\Delta\ell_2=0.
\]
Therefore we can rewrite \eqref{eq:10.1.11} and \eqref{eq:10.1.12}
as
\begin{align*}
m_1y_1''&=-(c_1+c_2)y_1'+c_2y_2'-(k_1+k_2)y_1+k_2y_2+F_1\\
m_2y_2''&=c_2y_1'-c_2y_2'+k_2y_1-k_2y_2+F_2.  \quad\bbox
\end{align*}


\begin{example}\label{example:10.1.3}
Let $\vect{X}=\vect{X}(t)=x(t)\,\ivect+y(t)\,\jvect+z(t)\,\vect{k}$ be
the position vector at time $t$ of an object with mass $m$, relative
to a rectangular coordinate system with origin at Earth's center
(\zcref{figure:10.1.3}). According to Newton's law of gravitation,
Earth's gravitational force $\vect{F}=\vect{F}(x,y,z)$ on the object is
inversely proportional to the square of the distance of the object
from Earth's center, and directed toward the center;   thus,
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.1.13}
\vect{F}=\frac{K}{\|\vect{X}\|^2}\left(-\frac{\vect{X}}{\|\vect{X}\|}\right)
=-K\frac{x\,\ivect+y\,\jvect+z\,\vect{k}}{\left(x^2+y^2+z^2\right)^{3/2}},
\end{equation}
where $K$ is a constant.  To determine $K$,  we observe that the magnitude
of $\vect{F}$  is
\[
\|\vect{F}\|=K\frac{\|\vect{X}\|}{\|\vect{X}\|^3}=\frac{K}{\|\vect{X}\|^2}
=\frac{K}{(x^2+y^2+z^2)}.
\]
Let $R$  be Earth's radius.
Since $\|\vect{F}\|=mg$ when the object is at Earth's
surface,
\[
mg = \frac{K}{R^2},\quad\text{so}\quad K=mgR^2.
\]
Therefore we can rewrite \eqref{eq:10.1.13} as
\[
\vect{F}
=-mgR^2\frac{x\,\ivect+y\,\jvect+z\,\vect{k}}{\left(x^2+y^2+z^2\right)^{3/2}}.
\]
Now suppose $\vect{F}$ is the only force acting on the object.
According to Newton's second law of motion, $\vect{F}=m\vect{X}''$;   that
is,
\[
 m(x''\,\ivect+y''\,\jvect+z''\,\vect{k})=
-mgR^2\frac{x\,\ivect+y\,\jvect+z\,\vect{k}}{\left(x^2+y^2+z^2\right)^{3/2}}.
\]
Cancelling the common factor $m$ and equating components on the two sides
of this equation yields the  system
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.1.14}
\begin{aligned}
 x''&=-\dfrac{gR^2x}{(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\\
 y''&=-\dfrac{gR^2y}{(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\\
 z''&=-\dfrac{gR^2z}{(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{3/2}}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
\end{example}

\boxit{Rewriting Higher Order  Systems as First Order Systems}

A system of the form
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.1.15}
\begin{aligned}
y_1'&=g_1(t,y_1,y_2,\dots,y_n)\\
y_2'&=g_2(t,y_1,y_2,\dots,y_n)\\
&\vdots\\
y_n'&=g_n(t,y_1,y_2,\dots,y_n)
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
is called a \emph{\color{blue}first order system}, since the only derivatives
occurring in it are first derivatives. The derivative of
each of the unknowns may depend upon the independent variable and all
the unknowns, but not on the derivatives of other unknowns. When we
wish to emphasize the number of unknown functions in \eqref{eq:10.1.15} we
will say that \eqref{eq:10.1.15} is an $n\times n$ system.

Systems involving higher order derivatives can often be reformulated
as first order systems by introducing additional unknowns. The next
two examples illustrate this.

\begin{example}\label{example:10.1.4}
Rewrite the system
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.1.16}
\begin{aligned}
m_1y_1''&=-(c_1+c_2)y_1'+c_2y_2'-(k_1+k_2)y_1+k_2y_2+F_1\\
m_2y_2''&=c_2y_1'-c_2y_2'+k_2y_1-k_2y_2+F_2.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
derived in \zcref{example:10.1.2}  as a system of first order
equations.
\end{example}

\solution{}
If we define $v_1=y_1'$ and $v_2=y_2'$,  then $v_1'=y_1''$ and
$v_2'=y_2''$,  so \eqref{eq:10.1.16} becomes
\begin{align*}
m_1v_1'&=-(c_1+c_2)v_1+c_2v_2-(k_1+k_2)y_1+k_2y_2+F_1\\
m_2v_2'&=c_2v_1-c_2v_2+k_2y_1-k_2y_2+F_2.
\end{align*}
Therefore $\{y_1,y_2,v_1,v_2\}$  satisfies the $4\times4$ first order
system
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.1.17}
\begin{aligned}
y_1'&=v_1\\
y_2'&=v_2\\
v_1'&=\dfrac{1}{m_1}\left[-(c_1+c_2)v_1+c_2v_2-(k_1+k_2)y_1+k_2y_2+F_1\right]\\
v_2'&=\dfrac{1}{m_2}\left[c_2v_1-c_2v_2+k_2y_1-k_2y_2+F_2\right].
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}


\color{blue}
\remark{ The difference in form between \eqref{eq:10.1.15} and
\eqref{eq:10.1.17}, due to the way in which the unknowns are \emph{\color{blue}denoted}
in the two systems, isn't  important;     \eqref{eq:10.1.17} is a first
order system, in that each equation in \eqref{eq:10.1.17} expresses the
first derivative of one of the unknown functions in a way that does
not involve derivatives of any of the other unknowns.}
\color{black}

\begin{example}\label{example:10.1.5}
 Rewrite the system
\begin{align*}
x''&=f(t,x,x',y,y',y'')\\
y'''&=g(t,x,x',y,y'y'')
\end{align*}
 as a first order system.
\end{example}

\solution
We regard $x$, $x'$, $y$, $y'$, and $y''$ as unknown functions, and
rename them
\[
x=x_1,\quad x'=x_2,\qquad y=y_1,\quad y'=y_2,\quad y''=y_3.
\]
These unknowns satisfy the system
\begin{align*}
x_1'&=x_2\\
x_2'&=f(t,x_1,x_2,y_1,y_2,y_3)\\
y_1'&=y_2\\
y_2'&=y_3\\
y_3'&=g(t,x_1,x_2,y_1,y_2,y_3).
\end{align*}


\boxit{Rewriting Scalar Differential Equations as Systems}

In this chapter we'll refer to differential equations involving only
one unknown function as \emph{\color{blue}scalar} differential equations. Scalar
differential equations can be rewritten as systems of first order
equations by the method illustrated in the next two examples.

\begin{example}\label{example:10.1.6}
Rewrite the equation
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.1.18}
y^{(4)}+4y'''+6y''+4y'+y=0
\end{equation}
as a $4\times4$ first order system.
\end{example}


\solution
We regard $y$, $y'$, $y''$, and $y'''$  as unknowns and rename them
\[
y=y_1,\quad y'=y_2,\quad y''=y_3,\quad\text{and}\quad  y'''=y_4.
\]
Then $y^{(4)}=y_4'$, so \eqref{eq:10.1.18} can be written as
\[
y_4'+4y_4+6y_3+4y_2+y_1=0.
\]
Therefore $\{y_1,y_2,y_3,y_4\}$ satisfies the system
\begin{align*}
y_1'&=y_2\\
y_2'&=y_3 \\
y_3'&=y_4\\
y_4'&=-4y_4-6y_3-4y_2-y_1.\bbox
\end{align*}

\begin{example}\label{example:10.1.7}
 Rewrite
\[
x'''=f(t,x,x',x'')
\]
as a system of first order equations.
\end{example}

\solution
We regard $x$, $x'$, and $x''$ as unknowns and rename them
\[
x=y_1, \quad x'=y_2,\quad\text{and}\quad  x''=y_3.
\]
Then
\[
y_1'=x'=y_2,\quad y_2'=x''=y_3,\quad\text{and}\quad  y_3'=x'''.
\]
Therefore $\{y_1,y_2,y_3\}$ satisfies the first order system
\begin{align*}
y_1'&=y_2\\
y_2'&=y_3\\
y_3'&=f(t,y_1,y_2,y_3).
\end{align*}

Since systems of differential equations involving higher derivatives
can be rewritten as first order systems by the method used in
\zcref[range]{example:10.1.5,example:10.1.7} , we'll consider only first
order systems.


\boxit{Numerical Solution of Systems}

The  numerical methods that we studied in \zcref{ch:numerical}
can be extended to systems, and most differential equation software
packages include programs to solve systems of equations. We won't go
into detail on numerical methods for systems;     however, for
illustrative purposes we'll describe  the Runge-Kutta method for
the numerical solution of  the initial value problem
\begin{align*}
y_1'&=g_1(t,y_1,y_2),\quad y_1(t_0)=y_{10},\\
y_2'&=g_2(t,y_1,y_2),\quad y_2(t_0)=y_{20}
\end{align*}
at equally spaced points $t_0$, $t_1$, \dots, $t_n=b$ in an interval
$[t_0,b]$. Thus,
\[
t_i=t_0+ih,\quad i=0,1,\dots,n,
\]
where
\[
h=\frac{b-t_0}{n}.
\]
We'll denote the approximate values of $y_1$ and $y_2$ at these
points
by $y_{1,0},y_{1,1},\dots,y_{1,n}$ and
 $y_{2,0},y_{2,1},\dots,y_{2,n}$.
The Runge-Kutta method computes these approximate values as follows:
given $y_{1,i}$ and $y_{2,i}$, compute
\begin{align*}
I_{1,i}&=g_1(t_i,y_{1,i},y_{2,i}),\\
J_{1,i}&=g_2(t_i,y_{1,i},y_{2,i}),\\
I_{2,i}&=g_1\left(t_i+\frac{h}{2},y_{1,i}+\frac{h}{2}I_{1,i},y_{2,i}+\frac{h}{2}J_{1,i}\right),\\
J_{2,i}&=g_2\left(t_i+\frac{h}{2},y_{1,i}+\frac{h}{2}I_{1,i},y_{2,i}+\frac{h}{2}J_{1,i}\right),\\
I_{3,i}&=g_1\left(t_i+\frac{h}{2},y_{1,i}+\frac{h}{2}I_{2,i},y_{2,i}+\frac{h}{2}J_{2,i}\right),\\
J_{3,i}&=g_2\left(t_i+\frac{h}{2},y_{1,i}+\frac{h}{2}I_{2,i},y_{2,i}+\frac{h}{2}J_{2,i}\right),\\
I_{4,i}&=g_1(t_i+h,y_{1,i}+hI_{3,i},y_{2,i}+hJ_{3,i}),\\
J_{4,i}&=g_2(t_i+h,y_{1,i}+hI_{3,i},y_{2,i}+hJ_{3,i}),
\end{align*}
and
\begin{align*}
y_{1,i+1}&=y_{1,i}+\frac{h}{6}(I_{1,i}+2I_{2,i}+2I_{3,i}+I_{4,i}),\\
y_{2,i+1}&=y_{2,i}+\frac{h}{6}(J_{1,i}+2J_{2,i}+2J_{3,i}+J_{4,i})
\end{align*}
for $i=0$, \dots, $n-1$. Under appropriate conditions on $g_1$ and $g_2$,
it can be shown that the global truncation error for the Runge-Kutta
method is $O(h^4)$, as in the scalar case considered in
\zcref{sec:runge_kutta}.

\section{Linear Systems of Differential  Equations}\label{sec:linear_systems}

A  first order system of  differential equations that can be written in
the form
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.2.1}
\begin{aligned}
y'_1&=a_{11}(t)y_1+a_{12}(t)y_2+\cdots+a_{1n}(t)y_n+f_1(t)\\
y'_2&=a_{21}(t)y_1+a_{22}(t)y_2+\cdots+a_{2n}(t)y_n+f_2(t)\\
&\vdots\\
y'_n&=a_{n1}(t)y_1+a_{n2}(t)y_2+\cdots+a_{nn}(t)y_n+f_n(t)
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
is called a \emph{\color{blue}linear system}.

The linear system \eqref{eq:10.2.1}  can be written in matrix form as
\[
\col{y'}n=\matfunc ann\col yn+\colfunc fn,
\]
or more briefly as
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.2.2}
\vect{y}'=A(t)\vect{y}+\vect{F}(t),
\end{equation}
where
\[
\vect{y}=\col yn,\quad
A(t)=\matfunc ann,\quad\text{and}\quad \vect{F}(t)=\colfunc fn.
\]
We call $A$  the \emph{\color{blue}coefficient matrix} of \eqref{eq:10.2.2} and
$\vect{F}$  the \emph{\color{blue}forcing function}.   We'll say that $A$
and $\vect{F}$ are \emph{\color{blue}continuous} if their entries are continuous.
If $\vect{F}=\vect{0}$, then \eqref{eq:10.2.2} is
\emph{\color{blue}homogeneous};
otherwise, \eqref{eq:10.2.2} is \emph{\color{blue}nonhomogeneous}.

An  initial value problem  for \eqref{eq:10.2.2} consists of
finding a solution of \eqref{eq:10.2.2} that equals a given constant
vector
\[
\vect{k} =\col kn.
\]
at some initial point $t_0$. We write this initial value problem as
\[
\vect{y}'=A(t)\vect{y}+\vect{F}(t), \quad  \vect{y}(t_0)=\vect{k}.
\]

The next theorem gives sufficient conditions for the existence
of solutions of initial value problems for \eqref{eq:10.2.2}. We omit the
proof.

\begin{theorem}\color{blue} \label{thmtype:10.2.1}
Suppose the coefficient matrix $A$ and the forcing function $\vect{f}$ are continuous on $(a,b)$, let $t_0$ be in $(a,b)$, and let $\vect{k}$ be an arbitrary constant $n$-vector. Then the initial value
problem
\[
\vect{y}'=A(t)\vect{y}+\vect{F}(t), \quad  \vect{y}(t_0)=\vect{k}
\]
 has a unique solution on $(a,b)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{example}\label{example:10.2.1}
\begin{alist}
\item % (a)
Write the system
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.2.3}
\begin{aligned}
y_1'&=\phantom{2}y_1+2y_2+2e^{4t} \\[1\jot]
y_2'&=2y_1+\phantom{2}y_2+\phantom{2}e^{4t}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
in matrix form and conclude from \zcref{thmtype:10.2.1} that every
initial value problem for \eqref{eq:10.2.3} has a unique solution on
$(-\infty,\infty)$.
\item % (b)
Verify that
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.2.4}
\vect{y}=
\frac{1}{5}\twocol87e^{4t}+c_1\twocol11e^{3t}+c_2\twocol1{-1}e^{-t}
\end{equation}
is a solution of \eqref{eq:10.2.3} for all values of the constants $c_1$
and $c_2$.
\item % (c)
Find the  solution of the initial value problem
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.2.5}
\vect{y}'=\twobytwo1221\vect{y}+\twocol21e^{4t},\quad  \vect{y}(0)=\frac{1}{5}\twocol3{22}.
\end{equation}
\end{alist}
\end{example}

\begin{alistSolution}
\item
The system  \eqref{eq:10.2.3} can be written in matrix form as
\[
\vect{y}'=\twobytwo1221\vect{y}+\twocol21e^{4t}.
\]
An initial value problem for \eqref{eq:10.2.3} can be written as
\[
\vect{y}'=\twobytwo1221\vect{y}+\twocol21e^{4t}, \quad
y(t_0)=\twocol{k_1}{k_2}.
\]
Since the coefficient matrix and the forcing function are both
continuous on $(-\infty,\infty)$, \zcref{thmtype:10.2.1} implies that
this problem has a unique solution on $(-\infty,\infty)$.

\item
If $\vect{y}$ is given by \eqref{eq:10.2.4}, then
\begin{align*}
A\vect{y}+\vect{F}&=
\frac{1}{5}\twobytwo1221\twocol87e^{4t}+
c_1\twobytwo1221\twocol11e^{3t}
%\\[2\jot]&&
+c_2\twobytwo1221\twocol1{-1}e^{-t}
+\twocol21e^{4t}\\[2\jot]
&=\frac{1}{5}\twocol{22}{23}e^{4t}+c_1\twocol33e^{3t}+c_2\twocol{-1}1e^{-t}
+\twocol21e^{4t}\\[2\jot]
&=\frac{1}{5}\twocol{32}{28}e^{4t}+3c_1\twocol11e^{3t}-c_2\twocol1{-1}e^{-t}
=\vect{y}'.
\end{align*}

\item
We must choose $c_1$ and $c_2$ in \eqref{eq:10.2.4} so that
\[
\frac{1}{5}\twocol87+c_1\twocol11+c_2\twocol1{-1}=\frac{1}{5}\twocol3{22},
\]
which is equivalent to
\[
\twobytwo111{-1}\twocol{c_1}{c_2}=\twocol{-1}3.
\]
Solving this system yields $c_1=1$, $c_2=-2$, so
\[
\vect{y}=\frac{1}{5}\twocol87e^{4t}+\twocol11e^{3t}-2\twocol1{-1}e^{-t}
\]
is the solution of  \eqref{eq:10.2.5}.
\end{alistSolution}

{
\color{blue}
\remark{The theory of $n\times n$ linear systems of differential
equations is analogous to the theory of the scalar $n$-th order
equation
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.2.6}
P_0(t)y^{(n)}+P_1(t)y^{(n-1)}+\cdots+P_n(t)y=F(t),
\end{equation}
as
developed in \zcref{sec:higher_theory}. For example, by rewriting
\eqref{eq:10.2.6} as an equivalent linear system it can be shown that
\zcref{thmtype:10.2.1} implies \zcref{thmtype:9.1.1}
(\zcref{exer:10.2.12}).}
}

\section{Basic Theory of Homogeneous Linear System}\label{sec:system_theory}

In this section we consider homogeneous linear systems $\vect{y}'=
A(t)\vect{y}$, where $A=A(t)$ is a continuous $n\times n$ matrix
function on an interval $(a,b)$. The theory of linear homogeneous
systems has much in common with the theory of linear homogeneous
scalar equations, which we considered in
\zcref{sec:linar_first_order,sec:homog_linear,sec:higher_theory}.

Whenever we refer to solutions of $\vect{y}'=A(t)\vect{y}$ we'll mean
solutions on $(a,b)$. Since $\vect{y}\equiv\vect{0}$ is obviously a
solution of $\vect{y}'=A(t)\vect{y}$, we call it the \emph{\color{blue}trivial}
solution. Any other solution is \emph{\color{blue}nontrivial}.

If $\vect{y}_1$, $\vect{y}_2$, \dots, $\vect{y}_n$ are vector functions
defined on an interval $(a,b)$ and $c_1$, $c_2$, \dots, $c_n$ are
constants, then
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.3.1}
\vect{y}=c_1\vect{y}_1+c_2\vect{y}_2+\cdots+c_n\vect{y}_n
\end{equation}
is a \emph{\color{blue}linear combination of} $\vect{y}_1$, $\vect{y}_2$,
\ldots, $\vect{y}_n$. It's easy show that if $\vect{y}_1$, $\vect{y}_2$,
\dots, $\vect{y}_n$ are solutions of $\vect{y}'=A(t)\vect{y}$ on $(a,b)$, then so is any linear combination of
 $\vect{y}_1$, $\vect{y}_2$, \dots, $\vect{y}_n$ (\zcref{exer:10.3.1}). We say that
$\{\vect{y}_1,\vect{y}_2,\dots,\vect{y}_n\}$ is a \emph{\color{blue}fundamental set of
solutions of $\vect{y}'=A(t)\vect{y}$ on} $(a,b)$ on if every solution of
$\vect{y}'=A(t)\vect{y}$ on $(a,b)$ can be written as a linear combination of
$\vect{y}_1$, $\vect{y}_2$, \dots, $\vect{y}_n$, as in \eqref{eq:10.3.1}.
In this
case we say that \eqref{eq:10.3.1} is the \emph{\color{blue}general solution of $\vect{y}'=A(t)\vect{y}$ on} $(a,b)$.



It can be shown that if $A$ is continuous on $(a,b)$ then $\vect{y}'=A(t)\vect{y}$ has infinitely many fundamental sets of solutions on
$(a,b)$ (\zcref{exer:10.3.15,exer:10.3.16}). The next
definition will help to characterize fundamental sets of solutions of
$\vect{y}'=A(t)\vect{y}$.


We say that a set $\{\vect{y}_1,\vect{y}_2,\dots,\vect{y}_n\}$ of
$n$-vector functions is \emph{\color{blue}linearly independent} on $(a,b)$ if the
only constants $c_1$, $c_2$, \dots, $c_n$ such that
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.3.2}
 c_1\vect{y}_1(t)+c_2\vect{y}_2(t)+\cdots+c_n\vect{y}_n(t)=0,\quad
a<t<b,
\end{equation}
are $c_1=c_2=\cdots=c_n=0$. If \eqref{eq:10.3.2} holds for some set of
constants $c_1$, $c_2$, \dots, $c_n$ that are not all zero, then $\{\vect{y}_1,\vect{y}_2,\dots,\vect{y}_n\}$ is \emph{\color{blue}linearly dependent} on
$(a,b)$

The next theorem is analogous to
\zcref{thmtype:5.1.3,thmtype:9.1.2}.

\begin{theorem}\color{blue}\label{thmtype:10.3.1}
Suppose the $n\times n$ matrix $A=A(t)$ is continuous on $(a,b)$.
Then a set
$\{\vect{y}_1,\vect{y}_2,\dots,\vect{y}_n\}$ of $n$ solutions of $\vect{y}'=A(t)\vect{y}$ on $(a,b)$ is a fundamental set if and only if it's
linearly independent on $(a,b)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{example}\label{example:10.3.1}
Show that the vector functions
\[
\vect{y}_1=\begin{bmatrix}e^t\\0\\e^{-t}\end{bmatrix},\quad
\vect{y}_2=\begin{bmatrix}0\\e^{3t}\\1\end{bmatrix},
\quad\text{and}\quad
\vect{y}_3=\begin{bmatrix}e^{2t}\\e^{3t}\\0\end{bmatrix}
\]
are linearly independent on every interval  $(a,b)$.
\end{example}

\solution
Suppose
\[
c_1\begin{bmatrix}{c}e^t\\0\\e^{-t}\end{bmatrix}+
c_2\begin{bmatrix}0\\e^{3t}\\1\end{bmatrix}+
c_3\begin{bmatrix}e^{2t}\\e^{3t}\\0\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\0\end{bmatrix},\quad a<t<b.
\]
We must show that $c_1=c_2=c_3=0$. Rewriting this equation in matrix
form yields
\[
\begin{bmatrix}e^t&0&e^{2t}\\0&e^{3t}&e^{3t}\\e^{-t}&1&0\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}c_1\\c_2\\c_3\end{bmatrix}=
\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\0\end{bmatrix},\quad a<t<b.
\]
Expanding the determinant of this system in cofactors of the entries
of the first row yields
\begin{align*}
\begin{vmatrix}e^t&0&e^{2t}\\0&e^{3t}&e^{3t}\\e^{-t}&1&0
\end{vmatrix}
&=e^t\begin{vmatrix}e^{3t}&e^{3t}\\1&0\end{vmatrix}
-0\begin{vmatrix}0&e^{3t}\\e^{-t}&0\end{vmatrix}
+e^{2t}\begin{vmatrix}0&e^{3t}\\e^{-t}&1\end{vmatrix} \\
&=e^t(-e^{3t})+e^{2t}(-e^{2t})=-2e^{4t}.
\end{align*}
Since this determinant is never zero,
$c_1=c_2=c_3=0$. \bbox


We can use the method in
\zcref{example:10.3.1}  to test
$n$ solutions $\{\vect{y}_1,\vect{y}_2,\dots,\vect{y}_n\}$ of any
$n\times n$ system
$\vect{y}'=A(t)\vect{y}$  for linear independence on an interval $(a,b)$
on which $A$ is continuous.  To explain this (and for other purposes
later), it's useful to write a linear combination of
$\vect{y}_1$, $\vect{y}_2$, \dots, $\vect{y}_n$ in a different way. We first
write the vector functions in terms of their components as
\[
\vect{y}_1
=\begin{bmatrix} y_{11}\\y_{21}\\ \vdots\\y_{n1}\end{bmatrix},\quad
\vect{y}_2
=\begin{bmatrix} y_{12}\\y_{22}\\ \vdots\\y_{n2}\end{bmatrix},\dots,\quad
\vect{y}_n=\begin{bmatrix} y_{1n}\\y_{2n}\\ \vdots\\ y_{nn}\end{bmatrix}.
\]
If
\[
\vect{y}=c_1\vect{y}_1+c_2\vect{y}_2+\cdots+c_n\vect{y}_n
\]
then
\begin{align*}
\vect{y}
&=c_1\begin{bmatrix} y_{11}\\y_{21}\\ \vdots\\y_{n1}\end{bmatrix}
+c_2\begin{bmatrix} y_{12}\\y_{22}\\ \vdots\\y_{n2}\end{bmatrix}+\cdots
+c_n\begin{bmatrix} y_{1n}\\y_{2n}\\ \vdots\\y_{nn}\end{bmatrix}\\
&=\begin{bmatrix}
y_{11}&y_{12}&\cdots&y_{1n} \\
y_{21}&y_{22}&\cdots&y_{2n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots \\
y_{n1}&y_{n2}&\cdots&y_{nn} \\
\end{bmatrix}\col cn.
\end{align*}
This shows that
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.3.3}
c_1\vect{y}_1+c_2\vect{y}_2+\cdots+c_n\vect{y}_n=Y\vect{c},
\end{equation}
where
\[
\vect{c}=\col cn
\]
and
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.3.4}
Y=[\vect{y}_1\; \vect{y}_2\; \cdots\; \vect{y}_n]=
\begin{bmatrix}
y_{11}&y_{12}&\cdots&y_{1n} \\
y_{21}&y_{22}&\cdots&y_{2n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots \\
y_{n1}&y_{n2}&\cdots&y_{nn} \\
\end{bmatrix};
\end{equation}
 that is, the columns of $Y$
are the vector functions $\vect{y}_1,\vect{y}_2,\dots,\vect{y}_n$.

For reference below, note that
\begin{align*}
Y'&=[\vect{y}_1'\; \vect{y}_2'\; \cdots\; \vect{y}_n']\\
&=[A\vect{y}_1\; A\vect{y}_2\; \cdots\; A\vect{y}_n]\\
&=A[\vect{y}_1\; \vect{y}_2\; \cdots\; \vect{y}_n]=AY;
\end{align*}
that is, $Y$ satisfies the matrix differential equation
\[
Y'=AY.
\]

The determinant of $Y$,
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.3.5}
W=\begin{vmatrix}
y_{11}&y_{12}&\cdots&y_{1n} \\
y_{21}&y_{22}&\cdots&y_{2n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots \\
y_{n1}&y_{n2}&\cdots&y_{nn} \\
\end{vmatrix}
\end{equation}
is called the
\href{http://www-history.mcs.st-and.ac.uk/Mathematicians/Wronski.html}
{\color{blue}\itshape Wronskian\/} of $\{\vect{y}_1,\vect{y}_2,\dots,\vect{y}_n\}$. It can be shown (\zcref{exer:10.3.2,exer:10.3.3})
that this definition is analogous to definitions of the Wronskian of
scalar functions given in \zcref{sec:homog_linear,sec:higher_theory}.
The next theorem is analogous to
\zcref{thmtype:5.1.4,thmtype:9.1.3}. The proof is sketched in
\zcref{exer:10.3.4} for
$n=2$ and in \zcref{exer:10.3.5} for general~$n$.

\begin{theorem}\color{blue}$[$Abel's Formula$]$ \label{thmtype:10.3.2}
Suppose the $n\times n$ matrix $A=A(t)$ is continuous on $(a,b),$ let
$\vect{y}_1$, $\vect{y}_2$, \dots, $\vect{y}_n$ be solutions of $\vect{y}'=A(t)\vect{y}$ on $(a,b),$ and let $t_0$ be in $(a,b)$. Then the
Wronskian of $\{\vect{y}_1,\vect{y}_2,\dots,\vect{y}_n\}$ is given by
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.3.6}
W(t)=W(t_0)\exp\left(
\int^t_{t_0}\big[a_{11}(s)+a_{22}(s)+\cdots+a_{nn}(s)]\,
ds\right), \quad  a < t < b.
\end{equation}
Therefore,  either $W$ has no zeros in  $(a,b)$ or $W\equiv0$
on  $(a,b).$
\end{theorem}

{
\color{blue}
\remark{
The sum of the diagonal entries of a square matrix $A$ is called the
\emph{\color{blue}trace} of $A$, denoted by tr$(A)$. Thus, for an $n\times n$
matrix $A$,
\[
\tr(A)=a_{11}+a_{22}+\cdots+a_{nn},
\]
and  \eqref{eq:10.3.6} can be written as
\[
W(t)=W(t_0)\exp\left(
\int^t_{t_0}\tr(A(s))\,
ds\right), \quad a < t < b.
\]}
}

The next theorem is analogous to
\zcref{thmtype:5.1.6,thmtype:9.1.4}.

\begin{theorem}\color{blue}\label{thmtype:10.3.3}
Suppose the  $n\times n$ matrix $A=A(t)$ is continuous
 on $(a,b)$ and let
$\vect{y}_1$, $\vect{y}_2$, \dots, $\vect{y}_n$
be  solutions of $\vect{y}'=A(t)\vect{y}$ on  $(a,b)$.
 Then the following statements are equivalent; that is, they are
either all true or all false:
\begin{alist}
\item % (a)
The general solution of $\vect{y}'=A(t)\vect{y}$ on  $(a,b)$ is
$\vect{y}=c_1\vect{y}_1+c_2\vect{y}_2+\cdots+c_n\vect{y}_n$,
where $c_1$, $c_2$, \dots, $c_n$  are arbitrary constants.
\item % (b)
  $\{\vect{y}_1,\vect{y}_2,\dots,\vect{y}_n\}$ is a fundamental
set of solutions of $\vect{y}'=A(t)\vect{y}$  on $(a,b)$.
\item % (c)
 $\{\vect{y}_1,\vect{y}_2,\dots,\vect{y}_n\}$ is linearly
independent on $(a,b)$.
\item % (d)
The Wronskian of  $\{\vect{y}_1,\vect{y}_2,\dots,\vect{y}_n\}$ is nonzero
at some point in $(a,b)$.
\item % (e)
The Wronskian of  $\{\vect{y}_1,\vect{y}_2,\dots,\vect{y}_n\}$ is nonzero
at all points in $(a,b)$.
\end{alist}
\end{theorem}


We say that $Y$ in \eqref{eq:10.3.4} is a \emph{\color{blue}fundamental matrix} for
$\vect{y}'=A(t)\vect{y}$ if any (and therefore all) of the statements
(a)--(e) of \zcref{thmtype:10.3.2} are true for the
columns of $Y$. In this case, \eqref{eq:10.3.3} implies that the general
solution of $\vect{y}'=A(t)\vect{y}$ can be written as $\vect{y}=Y\vect{c}$, where $\vect{c}$ is an arbitrary constant $n$-vector.

\begin{example}\label{example:10.3.2}
The vector functions
\[
\vect{y}_1=\twocol{-e^{2t}}{2e^{2t}} \quad\text{and}\quad 
\vect{y}_2=\twocol{-e^{-t}}{\phantom{-}e^{-t}}
\]
are solutions of the constant coefficient system
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.3.7}
\vect{y}'=\twobytwo{-4}{-3} 65 \vect{y}
\end{equation}
on $(-\infty,\infty)$.  (Verify.)
\begin{alist}
\item % (a)
Compute the Wronskian of $\{\vect{y}_1,\vect{y}_2\}$
directly from the definition \eqref{eq:10.3.5}
\item % (b)
Verify Abel's formula \eqref{eq:10.3.6} for the Wronskian of
$\{\vect{y}_1,\vect{y}_2\}$.
\item % (c)
Find the general solution of \eqref{eq:10.3.7}.
\item % (d)
 Solve the initial value problem
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.3.8}
\vect{y}'=\twobytwo{-4}{-3}65 \vect{y}, \quad \vect{y}(0)=
\begin{bmatrix} 4 \\-5\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
\end{alist}
\end{example}

\begin{alistSolution}
\item
From \eqref{eq:10.3.5}
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.3.9}
W(t)=\begin{vmatrix}-e^{2t}&-e^{-t}\\2e^{2t}&e^{-t}\end{vmatrix}
=e^{2t}e^{-t}
\twobytwo{-1}{-1}21=e^t.
\end{equation}

\item  Here
\[
A=\twobytwo{-4}{-3} 65,
\]
so
tr$(A)=-4+5=1$. If $t_0$ is an arbitrary real number then
\eqref{eq:10.3.6}  implies that
\[
W(t)=W(t_0)\exp{\left(\int_{t_0}^t1\,ds\right)}=
\begin{vmatrix}
-e^{2t_0}&-e^{-t_0}\\
2e^{2t_0}&e^{-t_0}
\end{vmatrix}e^{(t-t_0)}
=e^{t_0}e^{t-t_0}=e^t,
\]
which is consistent with \eqref{eq:10.3.9}.

\item Since $W(t)\ne0$,
\zcref{thmtype:10.3.3} implies that $\{\vect{y}_1,\vect{y}_2\}$ is
a fundamental set of solutions  of \eqref{eq:10.3.7} and
\[
Y=\begin{bmatrix}-e^{2t}&-e^{-t}\\2e^{2t}&e^{-t}\end{bmatrix}
\]
is a fundamental matrix for \eqref{eq:10.3.7}.
 Therefore the general
solution of \eqref{eq:10.3.7} is
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.3.10}
\vect{y}=c_1\vect{y}_1+c_2\vect{y}_2=
c_1\twocol {-e^{2t}}{2e^{2t}}+c_2\twocol{-e^{-t}}{e^{-t}}
=\begin{bmatrix}-e^{2t}&-e^{-t}\\2e^{2t}&e^{-t}\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}c_1\\c_2\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

\item
Setting $t=0$ in \eqref{eq:10.3.10} and imposing the initial condition
in \eqref{eq:10.3.8} yields
\[
c_1\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\2\end{bmatrix}+c_2
\begin{bmatrix}-1 \\1\end{bmatrix}=
\begin{bmatrix} 4 \\-5\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Thus,
\begin{align*}
-c_1-c_2&=\phantom{-}4 \\
2c_1+c_2&=-5.
\end{align*}
The solution of this system is $c_1=-1$, $c_2=-3$.  Substituting these
values into  \eqref{eq:10.3.10} yields
\[
\vect{y}=-\begin{bmatrix}-e^{2t} \\ 2e^{2t}\end{bmatrix}
-3\begin{bmatrix}-e^{-t} \\ e^{-t}\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix} e^{2t}+3e^{-t} \\-2e^{2t}-3e^{-t}\end{bmatrix}
\]
as the solution of  \eqref{eq:10.3.8}.
\end{alistSolution}

\section{Constant Coefficient Homogeneous Systems I}
\label{sec:system_const_coeff_i}

We'll now begin our study of the homogeneous system
\begin{equation}\label{eq:10.4.1}
\vect{y}'=A\vect{y},
\end{equation}
where $A$ is an $n\times n$ constant matrix. Since $A$ is continuous
on $(-\infty,\infty)$, \zcref{thmtype:10.2.1}
implies
that all solutions of \eqref{eq:10.4.1} are defined on $(-\infty,\infty)$.
Therefore, when we speak of solutions of $\vect{y}'=A\vect{y}$, we'll
mean solutions on $(-\infty,\infty)$.

In this section we assume that all the eigenvalues of $A$ are real and
that $A$ has a set of $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors. In the
next two sections we consider the cases where some of the eigenvalues
of $A$ are complex, or where $A$ does not have $n$ linearly
independent eigenvectors.

In \zcref{example:10.3.2} we showed that the vector
functions
\[
\vect{y}_1=\twocol {-e^{2t}}{2e^{2t}}\quad\text{and}\quad 
\vect{y}_2=\twocol{-e^{-t}}{e^{-t}}
\]
form a fundamental set of solutions of the system
\begin{equation}\label{eq:10.4.2}
\vect{y}'=\twobytwo{-4}{-3}65 \vect{y},
\end{equation}
but we did not show how we obtained $\vect{y}_1$ and $\vect{y}_2$ in the
first place. To see how these solutions can be obtained we write
\eqref{eq:10.4.2} as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:10.4.3}
\begin{aligned}
y_1'&=-4y_1-3y_2\\y_2'&=\phantom{-}6y_1+5y_2
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
and look for solutions of the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:10.4.4}
y_1=x_1e^{\lambda t}\quad\text{and}\quad y_2=x_2e^{\lambda t},
\end{equation}
where $x_1$, $x_2$, and $\lambda$ are constants to be determined.
 Differentiating  \eqref{eq:10.4.4} yields
\[
y_1'=\lambda x_1e^{\lambda
t}\quad\text{and}\quad y_2'=\lambda x_2e^{\lambda t}.
\]
Substituting this and  \eqref{eq:10.4.4} into  \eqref{eq:10.4.3} and canceling
the common factor $e^{\lambda t}$ yields
\begin{align*}
-4x_1-3x_2&=\lambda x_1 \\
6 x_1+5x_2&=\lambda x_2.
\end{align*}
For a given $\lambda$, this is a homogeneous algebraic system, since it can
be rewritten as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:10.4.5}
\begin{aligned}
(-4-\lambda) x_1-3 x_2&=0\\
6 x_1+(5-\lambda) x_2&=0.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The trivial solution $x_1=x_2=0$ of this system isn't  useful, since
it corresponds to the trivial solution $y_1\equiv y_2\equiv0$ of
\eqref{eq:10.4.3}, which can't be part of a fundamental set of solutions
of \eqref{eq:10.4.2}. Therefore we consider only those values of $\lambda$
for which \eqref{eq:10.4.5} has nontrivial solutions. These are the values
of $\lambda$ for which the determinant of \eqref{eq:10.4.5} is zero;   that
is,
\begin{align*}
\begin{vmatrix}-4-\lambda&-3\\6&5-\lambda\end{vmatrix}
&=(-4-\lambda)(5-\lambda)+18\\
&=\lambda^2-\lambda-2\\
&=(\lambda-2)(\lambda+1)=0,
\end{align*}
which has the solutions $\lambda_1=2$ and $\lambda_2=-1$.

Taking $\lambda=2$ in  \eqref{eq:10.4.5} yields
\begin{align*}
-6 x_1-3 x_2&=0\\
6 x_1+3 x_2&=0,
\end{align*}
which implies that $x_1=-x_2/2$, where  $x_2$ can be
chosen arbitrarily. Choosing
$x_2=2$ yields the solution $y_1=-e^{2t}$,
$y_2=2e^{2t}$ of  \eqref{eq:10.4.3}. We can write this solution in vector
form as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:10.4.6}
\vect{y}_1=\twocol {-1}{\phantom{-}2} e^{2t}.
\end{equation}

Taking $\lambda=-1$ in  \eqref{eq:10.4.5} yields the system
\begin{align*}
-3 x_1-3 x_2&=0\\
\phantom{-}6 x_1+6 x_2&=0,
\end{align*}
so $x_1=-x_2$. Taking $x_2=1$ here
yields the solution $y_1=-e^{-t}$, $y_2=e^{-t}$ of  \eqref{eq:10.4.3}. We
can write this solution in vector form as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:10.4.7}
\vect{y}_2=\twocol{-1}{\phantom{-}1}e^{-t}.
\end{equation}

In \eqref{eq:10.4.6} and \eqref{eq:10.4.7} the constant coefficients in the
arguments of the exponential functions are the eigenvalues of the
coefficient matrix in \eqref{eq:10.4.2}, and the vector coefficients of the
exponential functions are associated eigenvectors. This illustrates
the next theorem.

\begin{theorem}\color{blue}\label{thmtype:10.4.1}
 Suppose the $n\times n$
constant matrix $A$ has $n$ real eigenvalues
$\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\ldots,\lambda_n$
(which need not be distinct) with associated
linearly independent eigenvectors $\vect{X}_1,\vect{X}_2,\ldots,\vect{X}_n$.
Then the functions
\[
\vect{y}_1=\vect{X}_1e^{\lambda_1 t},\,
 \vect{y}_2=\vect{X}_2e^{\lambda_2 t},\,
\dots,\,
\vect{y}_n=\vect{X}_ne^{\lambda_n t}
\]
 form a fundamental set of solutions of  $\vect{y}'=A\vect{y}$;
that is, the general solution of this system is
\[
\vect{y}=c_1\vect{X}_1e^{\lambda_1 t}+c_2\vect{X}_2e^{\lambda_2 t}
+\cdots+c_n\vect{X}_ne^{\lambda_n t}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Differentiating $\vect{y}_i=\vect{X}_ie^{\lambda_it}$ and recalling
that $A\vect{X}_i=\lambda_i\vect{X}_i$ yields
\[
\vect{y}_i'=\lambda_i\vect{X}_ie^{\lambda_it}=A\vect{X}_ie^{\lambda_it}
=A\vect{y}_i.
\]
This shows that $\vect{y}_i$ is a solution of $\vect{y}'=A\vect{y}$.

The Wronskian of
 $\{\vect{y}_1,\vect{y}_2,\ldots,\vect{y}_n\}$ is
\[
\begin{vmatrix}
x_{11}e^{\lambda_1 t}& x_{12}e^{\lambda_2 t}&\cdots& x_{1n}e^{\lambda_n t}\\
x_{21}e^{\lambda_1 t}& x_{22}e^{\lambda_2 t}&\cdots& x_{2n}e^{\lambda_n t}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
x_{n1}e^{\lambda_1 t}& x_{n2}e^{\lambda_2 t}&\cdots& x_{nn}e^{\lambda x_n t}
\end{vmatrix}
=e^{\lambda_1 t}e^{\lambda_2 t}\cdots e^{\lambda_n t}
\begin{vmatrix}
x_{11}&x_{12}&\cdots&x_{1n}\\
x_{21}&x_{22}&\cdots&x_{2n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
x_{n1}&x_{n2}&\cdots&x_{nn}
\end{vmatrix}.
\]
Since the columns of the determinant on the right are $\vect{X}_1$, $\vect{x}_2$, \dots, $\vect{X}_n$, which are assumed to be linearly independent,
the determinant is nonzero. Therefore
\zcref{thmtype:10.3.3} implies that
$\{\vect{y}_1,\vect{y}_2,\ldots,\vect{y}_n\}$ is a fundamental set of
solutions of $\vect{y}'=A\vect{y}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{example:10.4.1}
\begin{alist}
\item % (a)
 Find the general solution of
\begin{equation}\label{eq:10.4.8}
\vect{y}'=\twobytwo2442
\vect{y}.
\end{equation}

\item % (b)
 Solve the initial value problem
\begin{equation}\label{eq:10.4.9}
\vect{y}'=\twobytwo2442
\vect{y},\quad\vect{y}(0)=\begin{bmatrix}5 \\-1\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
\end{alist}
\end{example}

\begin{alistSolution}
\item The characteristic
polynomial of the  coefficient matrix $A$ in  \eqref{eq:10.4.8} is
\begin{align*}
\begin{vmatrix} 2-\lambda&4\\4&2-\lambda\end{vmatrix}
&= (\lambda-2)^2-16\\
&= (\lambda-2-4)(\lambda-2+4)\\
&= (\lambda-6)(\lambda+2).
\end{align*}
Hence,  $\lambda_1=6$ and $\lambda_2
=-2$ are eigenvalues of $A$. To obtain the eigenvectors,
we must solve the system
\begin{equation}\label{eq:10.4.10}
\begin{bmatrix} 2-\lambda&4\\4&2-\lambda\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} x_1\\x_2\end{bmatrix}=
\begin{bmatrix} 0\\0\end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
with $\lambda=6$ and $\lambda=-2$.  Setting $\lambda=6$ in
\eqref{eq:10.4.10} yields
 \[
\begin{bmatrix}-4&4\\4&-4\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}x_1\\x_2\end{bmatrix}=
\begin{bmatrix} 0\\0\end{bmatrix},
\]
which implies that $x_1=x_2$.  Taking $x_2=1$ yields the eigenvector
\[
\vect{X}_1=\begin{bmatrix} 1\\1\end{bmatrix},
\]
so
\[
\vect{y}_1=\begin{bmatrix} 1\\1\end{bmatrix}e^{6t}
\]
is a solution of  \eqref{eq:10.4.8}.  Setting $\lambda=-2$ in
\eqref{eq:10.4.10} yields
\[
\begin{bmatrix} 4&4\\4&4\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} x_1\\x_2\end{bmatrix}=
\begin{bmatrix} 0\\0\end{bmatrix},
\]
which implies that $x_1=-x_2$.  Taking $x_2=1$ yields the eigenvector
\[
\vect{X}_2=\begin{bmatrix}-1\\1\end{bmatrix},
\]
so
\[
\vect{y}_2=\begin{bmatrix}-1\\1\end{bmatrix}e^{-2t}
\]
is a solution of  \eqref{eq:10.4.8}.
From \zcref{thmtype:10.4.1}, the general solution of
\eqref{eq:10.4.8} is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:10.4.11}
\vect{y}=c_1\vect{y}_1+c_2\vect{y}_2=c_1\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}e^{6t}
+c_2\begin{bmatrix}-1\\1\end{bmatrix}e^{-2t}.
\end{equation}

\item
 To satisfy the initial condition in  \eqref{eq:10.4.9}, we must choose
$c_1$ and $c_2$ in  \eqref{eq:10.4.11} so that
\[
c_1\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}+c_2\begin{bmatrix}-1\\1\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}5\\-1\end{bmatrix}.
\]
This is equivalent to the system
\begin{align*}
c_1-c_2&=\phantom{-}5\\
c_1+c_2&=-1,
\end{align*}
so $c_1=2, c_2=-3$.  Therefore the solution of
\eqref{eq:10.4.9} is
\[
\vect{y}=2\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}e^{6t}-3
\begin{bmatrix}-1\\1\end{bmatrix}e^{-2t},
\]
or, in terms of components,
\[
y_1=2e^{6t}+3e^{-2t},\quad y_2=2e^{6t}-3e^{-2t}.
\]
\end{alistSolution}

\end{align*}
\end{alistSolution}

\begin{example}\label{example:10.4.3}
 Find the general solution of
\begin{equation}\label{eq:10.4.16}
\vect{y}'=\begin{bmatrix}-3&2&2\\2&-3&2\\2&2&-3\end{bmatrix}\vect{y}.
\end{equation}
\end{example}

\solution  The characteristic polynomial of
the  coefficient matrix $A$ in  \eqref{eq:10.4.16} is
\[
\begin{vmatrix}-3-\lambda&2&2\\2&-3-\lambda&2\\2&2&-3-\lambda\end{vmatrix}
=-(\lambda-1)(\lambda+5)^2.
\]
Hence, $\lambda_1=1$ is an eigenvalue of multiplicity $1$, while
$\lambda_2=-5$ is an eigenvalue of multiplicity $2$. Eigenvectors
associated with $\lambda_1=1$ are solutions of the system with
augmented matrix
\[
\amatrix{-4&2&2\\2&-4&2\\2&2&-4}{0\\0\\0},
\]
which is row equivalent to
\[
\amatrix{1&0&-1\\0&1&-1\\0&0&0}{0\\0\\0}.
\]
Hence, $x_1=x_2=x_3$, and we choose $x_3=1$ to obtain the solution
\begin{equation}\label{eq:10.4.17}
\vect{y}_1=\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{bmatrix}e^t
\end{equation}
of  \eqref{eq:10.4.16}.  Eigenvectors associated with $\lambda_2=-5$
are solutions of the system
with  augmented matrix
\[
\amatrix{2&2&2\\2&2&2\\2&2&2}{0\\0\\0}.
\]
Hence, the components of these eigenvectors need only satisfy the single
condition
\[
x_1+x_2+x_3=0.
\]
Since there's only one equation here, we can choose $x_2$ and
$x_3$  arbitrarily. We obtain one eigenvector by choosing $x_2=0$
and $x_3=1$, and another by choosing $x_2=1$ and $x_3=0$. In both
cases $x_1=-1$. Therefore
\[
\begin{bmatrix}-1\\0\\1\end{bmatrix}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\begin{bmatrix}-1\\1\\0\end{bmatrix}
\]
are linearly independent eigenvectors associated with  $\lambda_2=
-5$, and the corresponding solutions of  \eqref{eq:10.4.16} are
\[
\vect{y}_2=\begin{bmatrix}-1\\0\\1\end{bmatrix}e^{-5t}\quad
\text{and}\quad\vect{y}_3=\begin{bmatrix}-1\\1\\0\end{bmatrix}e^{-5t}.
\]
Because of this and  \eqref{eq:10.4.17}, \zcref{thmtype:10.4.1} implies
that  the general solution of \eqref{eq:10.4.16} is
\[
\vect{y}=c_1\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{bmatrix}e^t+c_2
\begin{bmatrix}-1\\0\\1\end{bmatrix}
e^{-5t}+c_3\begin{bmatrix}-1\\1\\0\end{bmatrix}e^{-5t}.
\]


\boxit{Geometric Properties of Solutions when  $n=2$}

We'll now  consider the geometric properties of solutions of a
$2\times2$ constant coefficient system
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.4.18}
\twocol{y_1'}{y_2'}=\begin{bmatrix}
a_{11}&a_{12}\\a_{21}&a_{22}
\end{bmatrix}\twocol{y_1}{y_2}.
\end{equation}
It is convenient to think of a ``$y_1$-$y_2$ plane," where a point
is identified by rectangular coordinates $(y_1,y_2)$. If $\vect{y}=\twocol{y_1}{y_2}$ is a non-constant solution of \eqref{eq:10.4.18},
then the point $(y_1(t),y_2(t))$ moves along a curve $C$ in the
$y_1$-$y_2$ plane as $t$ varies from $-\infty$ to $\infty$. We call
$C$ the \emph{\color{blue}trajectory} of $\vect{y}$. (We also say that $C$ is a
trajectory of the system \eqref{eq:10.4.18}.) I's important to note that
$C$ is the trajectory of infinitely many solutions of \eqref{eq:10.4.18},
since if $\tau$ is any real number, then $\vect{y}(t-\tau)$ is a
solution of \eqref{eq:10.4.18} (\zcref{exer:10.4.28}(b)), and
$(y_1(t-\tau),y_2(t-\tau))$ also moves along $C$ as $t$ varies from
$-\infty$ to $\infty$. Moreover, \zcref{exer:10.4.28}(c)
implies
that distinct trajectories of \eqref{eq:10.4.18} can't intersect, and that
two solutions $\vect{y}_1$ and $\vect{y}_2$ of \eqref{eq:10.4.18} have the
same trajectory if and only if $\vect{y}_2(t)=\vect{y}_1(t-\tau)$ for
some $\tau$.

From \zcref{exer:10.4.28}(a), a trajectory of a nontrivial
solution
of \eqref{eq:10.4.18} can't contain $(0,0)$, which we define to be the
trajectory of the trivial solution $\vect{y}\equiv0$. More generally,
if $\vect{y}=\twocol{k_1}{k_2}\ne\vect{0}$ is a constant solution
of \eqref{eq:10.4.18} (which could occur if zero is an eigenvalue of the
matrix of \eqref{eq:10.4.18}), we define the trajectory of $\vect{y}$ to be
the single point $(k_1,k_2)$.

To be specific, this is the question: What do the trajectories look
like, and how are they traversed? In this section we'll answer this
question, assuming that the matrix
\[
A=\begin{bmatrix}a_{11}&a_{12}\\a_{21}&a_{22}\end{bmatrix}
\]
of \eqref{eq:10.4.18} has real eigenvalues $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ with
associated linearly independent eigenvectors $\vect{X}_1$ and $\vect{x}_2$. Then  the general solution of \eqref{eq:10.4.18} is
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.4.19}
\vect{y}= c_1\vect{X}_1
 e^{\lambda_1 t}+c_2\vect{X}_2e^{\lambda_2 t}.
\end{equation}
We'll consider other situations in the next two sections.

We leave it to you (\zcref{exer:10.4.35}) to classify the
trajectories of \eqref{eq:10.4.18} if zero is an eigenvalue of $A$. We'll
confine our attention here to the case where both eigenvalues are
nonzero. In this case the simplest situation is where
$\lambda_1=\lambda_2\ne0$, so  \eqref{eq:10.4.19} becomes
\[
\vect{y}=(c_1\vect{X}_1+c_2\vect{X}_2)e^{\lambda_1 t}.
\]
Since $\vect{X}_1$ and
$\vect{X}_2$ are linearly independent, an arbitrary vector $\vect{X}$
can be written as $\vect{X}=c_1\vect{X}_1+c_2\vect{X}_2$. Therefore the
general solution of \eqref{eq:10.4.18} can be written as $\vect{y}=\vect{x}e^{\lambda_1 t}$ where $\vect{X}$ is an arbitrary $2$-vector, and the
trajectories of nontrivial solutions of \eqref{eq:10.4.18} are half-lines
through (but not including) the origin. The direction of motion is
away from the origin if $\lambda_1>0$ (\zcref{figure:10.4.1}),
toward it if $\lambda_1<0$ (\zcref{figure:10.4.2}). (In these
and the next figures an arrow through a point indicates the
direction of motion along the trajectory through the point.)

Now suppose   $\lambda_2>\lambda_1$, and let $L_1$ and $L_2$ denote
lines through the origin parallel to $\vect{X}_1$ and $\vect{X}_2$,
respectively. By a half-line of $L_1$ (or $L_2$), we mean either of the
rays obtained by removing the origin from $L_1$ (or $L_2$).

Letting $c_2=0$ in \eqref{eq:10.4.19} yields $\vect{y}=c_1\vect{x}_1e^{\lambda_1 t}$. If $c_1\ne0$, the trajectory defined by this
solution is a half-line of $L_1$. The direction of motion is away from
the origin if $\lambda_1>0$, toward the origin if $\lambda_1<0$.
Similarly, the trajectory of  $\vect{y}=c_2\vect{X}_2e^{\lambda_2 t}$
with $c_2\ne0$ is a half-line of $L_2$.

Henceforth, we assume that $c_1$ and $c_2$ in \eqref{eq:10.4.19} are both
nonzero. In this case, the trajectory of \eqref{eq:10.4.19} can't
intersect
$L_1$ or $L_2$, since every point on these lines is on the trajectory
of a solution for which either $c_1=0$ or $c_2=0$. (Remember: distinct
trajectories can't intersect!). Therefore the trajectory of
\eqref{eq:10.4.19} must lie entirely in one of the four open sectors
bounded by $L_1$ and $L_2$, but do not any point  on $L_1$ or
$L_2$. Since the initial point $(y_1(0),y_2(0))$ defined by
\[
\vect{y}(0)=c_1\vect{X}_1+c_2\vect{X}_2
\]
is on the trajectory, we can determine which sector contains the
trajectory from the signs of $c_1$ and $c_2$,
as shown in \zcref{figure:10.4.3}.



The direction of $\vect{y}(t)$ in \eqref{eq:10.4.19} is the
same as that of
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.4.20}
e^{-\lambda_2 t}\vect{y}(t)=
c_1\vect{X}_1e^{-(\lambda_2-\lambda_1)t}+c_2\vect{X}_2
\end{equation}
and of
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.4.21}
e^{-\lambda_1 t}\vect{y}(t)=c_1\vect{x}_1+c_2\vect{X}_2e^{(\lambda_2-\lambda_1)t}.
\end{equation}
Since the right side of \eqref{eq:10.4.20} approaches $c_2\vect{X}_2$ as
$t\to\infty$, the trajectory is asymptotically parallel to $L_2$ as
$t\to\infty$. Since the right side of \eqref{eq:10.4.21} approaches
$c_1\vect{X}_1$ as $t\to-\infty$, the trajectory is asymptotically
parallel to $L_1$ as $t\to-\infty$.

The shape and direction of
traversal of the trajectory of \eqref{eq:10.4.19} depend upon whether
$\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ are both positive, both negative, or of
opposite signs. We'll now analyze these three cases.

of competition is to assume that the growth rate per individual of
each population is reduced by an amount proportional to the other
population, so (A) is replaced by
\begin{align*}
P'&=\phantom{-}aP-\alpha Q\\
Q'&=-\beta P+bQ,
\end{align*}
where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are positive constants. (Since negative
population doesn't make sense, this system holds only while $P$ and
$Q$ are both positive.) Now suppose $P(0)=P_0>0$ and
$Q(0)=Q_0>0$.

\begin{alist}
\item % (a)
For several choices of $a$, $b$, $\alpha$, and $\beta$, verify
experimentally
(by graphing trajectories of (A) in the $P$-$Q$ plane) that there's a
constant $\rho>0$ (depending upon $a$, $b$, $\alpha$, and $\beta$) with the
following properties:
\begin{rmlist}
\item % (i)
If $Q_0>\rho P_0$, then $P$ decreases monotonically to zero
in finite time, during which $Q$ remains positive.
\item % (ii)
If $Q_0<\rho P_0$, then $Q$ decreases monotonically to zero in
finite time, during which $P$ remains positive.
\end{rmlist}
\item % (b)
Conclude from  (a) that exactly one of the species
becomes extinct in finite time if $Q_0\ne\rho P_0$. Determine
experimentally what happens if $Q_0=\rho P_0$.
\item % (c)
Confirm your experimental results and determine $\gamma$ by expressing
the eigenvalues and associated eigenvectors of
\[
A=\twobytwo a{-\alpha}{-\beta}b
\]
in terms of $a$, $b$, $\alpha$, and $\beta$, and applying the geometric
arguments developed at the end of this section.
\end{alist}

\end{exerciselist}


\section{Constant Coefficient Homogeneous Systems II}
\label{sec:system_const_coeff_ii}

We saw in \zcref{sec:system_const_coeff_i} that if an $n\times n$
constant matrix
$A$ has $n$ real eigenvalues $\lambda_1$, $\lambda_2$, \dots, $\lambda_n$
(which need not be distinct) with associated linearly independent
eigenvectors $\vect{X}_1$, $\vect{X}_2$, \dots, $\vect{X}_n$, then the general
solution of $\vect{y}'=A\vect{y}$ is
\[
\vect{y}=c_1\vect{X}_1e^{\lambda_1t}+c_2\vect{X}_2e^{\lambda_2 t}
+\cdots+c_n\vect{X}_ne^{\lambda_n t}.
\]
In this section we consider the case where $A$ has $n$ real
eigenvalues, but does not have $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors.
It is shown in linear algebra that this occurs if and only if $A$ has
at least one eigenvalue of multiplicity $r>1$ such that the associated
eigenspace has dimension less than $r$. In this case $A$ is said to be
\emph{\color{blue}defective}. Since it's beyond the scope of this book to give a
complete analysis of systems with defective coefficient matrices, we
will restrict our attention to some commonly occurring special cases.

\begin{example}\label{example:10.5.1}
Show that the system
\begin{equation}\label{eq:10.5.1}
\vect{y}'=\twobytwo{11}{-25}4{-9}\vect{y}
\end{equation}
does not have a fundamental set of solutions of the form $\{\vect{x}_1e^{\lambda_1t},\vect{X}_2e^{\lambda_2t}\}$, where $\lambda_1$ and
$\lambda_2$ are eigenvalues of the coefficient matrix $A$ of
\eqref{eq:10.5.1} and $\vect{X}_1$, and $\vect{X}_2$ are associated
linearly independent eigenvectors.
\end{example}

\solution   The characteristic polynomial of $A$ is
\begin{align*}
\twochar{11}{-25}4{-9}
&=(\lambda-11)(\lambda+9)+100\\
&=\lambda^2-2\lambda+1=(\lambda-1)^2.
\end{align*}
Hence, $\lambda=1$ is the only eigenvalue of $A$. The augmented
matrix of the system $(A-I)\vect{X}=\vect{0}$ is
\[
\amatrix{10&-25\\4&-10}{0\\0},
\]
which is row equivalent to
\[
\amatrix{1&-\frac52\\0&0}{0\\0}.
\]
Hence, $x_1=5x_2/2$ where $x_2$ is arbitrary. Therefore all
eigenvectors of $A$ are scalar multiples of $\vect{x}_1=\twocol52$,
so $A$ does not have a set of two linearly independent eigenvectors.
\bbox

From \zcref{example:10.5.1}, we know that all scalar multiples of
$\vect{y}_1=\twocol52e^t$ are solutions of \eqref{eq:10.5.1};
however,
to find the general solution we must find a second solution $\vect{y}_2$ such that $\{\vect{y}_1,\vect{y}_2\}$ is linearly independent.
Based on your recollection of the procedure for solving a constant
coefficient scalar equation
\[
ay''+by'+cy=0
\]
in the case where the characteristic polynomial has a repeated root,
you might expect to obtain a second solution of \eqref{eq:10.5.1} by
multiplying the first solution by $t$. However, this yields $\vect{y}_2=\twocol52te^t$, which doesn't work, since
\[
\vect{y}_2'=\twocol52(te^t+e^t),\quad\text{while}\quad
\twobytwo{11}{-25}4{-9}\vect{y}_2=\twocol52te^t.
\]

The next theorem shows what to do in this situation.

\begin{theorem}\color{blue}\label{thmtype:10.5.1}
Suppose the $n\times n$ matrix $A$ has an eigenvalue $\lambda_1$
of multiplicity $\ge2$ and the associated eigenspace has dimension
$1;$ that is, all $\lambda_1$-eigenvectors of $A$ are scalar
multiples
of  an eigenvector $\vect{X}.$  Then there are infinitely many vectors
$\vect{u}$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:10.5.2}
(A-\lambda_1I)\vect{u}=\vect{X}.
\end{equation}
Moreover, if $\vect{u}$ is any such vector  then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:10.5.3}
\vect{y}_1=\vect{X}e^{\lambda_1t}\quad\text{and}\quad
\vect{y}_2=\vect{u}e^{\lambda_1t}+\vect{X}te^{\lambda_1t}
\end{equation}
are linearly independent  solutions of $\vect{y}'=A\vect{y}.$
\end{theorem}

A complete proof of this theorem is beyond the scope of this book. The
difficulty is in proving that there's a vector $\vect{u}$ satisfying
\eqref{eq:10.5.2}, since $\det(A-\lambda_1I)=0$. We'll take this without
proof and verify the other assertions of the theorem.

We already know that $\vect{y}_1$ in \eqref{eq:10.5.3} is a solution of
$\vect{y}'=A\vect{y}$. To see that $\vect{y}_2$ is also a solution, we
compute
\begin{align*}
\vect{y}_2'-A\vect{y}_2
&=\lambda_1\vect{u}e^{\lambda_1t}+\vect{X} e^{\lambda_1t}
+\lambda_1\vect{X} te^{\lambda_1t}-A\vect{u}e^{\lambda_1t}
-A\vect{X} te^{\lambda_1t}\\
&=(\lambda_1\vect{u}+\vect{X} -A\vect{u})e^{\lambda_1t}
+(\lambda_1\vect{X} -A\vect{X} )te^{\lambda_1t}.
\end{align*}
Since $A\vect{X}=\lambda_1\vect{X}$, this can be written as
\[
\vect{y}_2'-A\vect{y}_2=-
\left((A-\lambda_1I)\vect{u}-\vect{X}\right)e^{\lambda_1t},
\]
and now \eqref{eq:10.5.2} implies that
$\vect{y}_2'=A\vect{y}_2$.

To see that $\vect{y}_1$ and $\vect{y}_2$  are linearly independent,
suppose   $c_1$ and $c_2$ are constants such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:10.5.4}
c_1\vect{y}_1+c_2\vect{y}_2=c_1\vect{X}e^{\lambda_1t}+c_2(\vect{u}e^{\lambda_1t} +\vect{X}te^{\lambda_1t})=\vect{0}.
\end{equation}
We must show that $c_1=c_2=0$.  Multiplying \eqref{eq:10.5.4}
by $e^{-\lambda_1t}$ shows that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:10.5.5}
c_1\vect{X}+c_2(\vect{u} +\vect{X}t)=\vect{0}.
\end{equation}
By differentiating this with respect to $t$, we see that $c_2\vect{x}=\vect{0}$, which implies $c_2=0$, because $\vect{X}\ne\vect{0}$.
Substituting  $c_2=0$ into \eqref{eq:10.5.5}  yields $c_1\vect{X}=\vect{0}$,
which implies that $c_1=0$, again because $\vect{X}\ne\vect{0}$

\begin{example}\label{example:10.5.2}
Use \zcref{thmtype:10.5.1} to find the general solution of the system
\begin{equation}\label{eq:10.5.6}
\vect{y}'=\twobytwo{11}{-25}4{-9}\vect{y}
\end{equation}
considered in \zcref{example:10.5.1}.
\end{example}

\solution In \zcref{example:10.5.1} we saw that $\lambda_1=1$ is an
eigenvalue of multiplicity $2$ of the coefficient matrix $A$ in
\eqref{eq:10.5.6}, and that all of the eigenvectors of $A$ are multiples of
\[
\vect{X}=\twocol52.
\]
Therefore
\[
\vect{y}_1=\twocol52e^t
\]
is a solution of \eqref{eq:10.5.6}. From \zcref{thmtype:10.5.1}, a second
solution is given by $\vect{y}_2=\vect{u}e^t+\vect{X}te^t$, where
$(A-I)\vect{u}=\vect{X}$. The augmented matrix of this system is
\[
\amatrix{10&-25\\4&-10}{5\\2},
\]
which is row equivalent to
\[
\amatrix{1&-\frac52\\0&0}{\frac12\\0}.
\]
Therefore the components of $\vect{u}$ must satisfy
\[
u_1-\frac{5}{2}u_2=\frac{1}{2},
\]
where  $u_2$ is arbitrary. We choose $u_2=0$, so that $u_1=1/2$ and
\[
\vect{u}=\twocol{\frac{1}{2}}0.
\]
Thus,
\[
\vect{y}_2=\twocol10\frac{e^t}{2}+\twocol52te^t.
\]
Since $\vect{y}_1$ and $\vect{y}_2$ are linearly independent by
\zcref{thmtype:10.5.1}, they form a fundamental set of solutions of
\eqref{eq:10.5.6}. Therefore the general solution of \eqref{eq:10.5.6} is
\[
\vect{y}=c_1\twocol52e^t+c_2\left(\twocol10\frac{e^t}{2}+\twocol52te^t\right).\bbox
\]

Note that choosing the arbitrary constant $u_2$ to be nonzero is
equivalent to adding a scalar multiple of $\vect{y}_1$ to the second
solution $\vect{y}_2$ (\zcref{exer:10.5.33}).

\begin{theorem}\color{blue}\label{thmtype:10.5.2}
Suppose the $n\times n$ matrix $A$ has an eigenvalue $\lambda_1$ of
multiplicity $\ge 3$ and the associated eigenspace is
one-dimensional; that is, all eigenvectors associated with
$\lambda_1$
are scalar multiples of the eigenvector $\vect{X}.$ Then there are
infinitely many vectors $\vect{u}$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:10.5.9}
(A-\lambda_1I)\vect{u}=\vect{X},
\end{equation}
and, if $\vect{u}$ is any such vector,  there are infinitely many
vectors $\vect{v}$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:10.5.10}
(A-\lambda_1I)\vect{v}=\vect{u}.
\end{equation}
 If $\vect{u}$ satisfies \eqref{eq:10.5.9}  and $\vect{v}$ satisfies
\eqref{eq:10.5.10},  then
\begin{align*}
\vect{y}_1&=\vect{X} e^{\lambda_1t},\\
\vect{y}_2&=\vect{u}e^{\lambda_1t}+\vect{X} te^{\lambda_1t},\quad\text{and}\\
\vect{y}_3&=\vect{v}e^{\lambda_1t}+\vect{u}te^{\lambda_1t}
+\vect{X} \frac{t^2e^{\lambda_1t}}{2}
\end{align*}
are linearly independent solutions of  $\vect{y}'=A\vect{y}$.
\end{theorem}

Again, it's beyond the scope of this book to prove that there are
vectors $\vect{u}$ and $\vect{v}$ that satisfy \eqref{eq:10.5.9} and
\eqref{eq:10.5.10}. \zcref[S]{thmtype:10.5.1} implies that $\vect{y}_1$ and
$\vect{y}_2$ are solutions of $\vect{y}'=A\vect{y}$. We leave the rest of
the proof to you (\zcref{exer:10.5.34}).

\amatrix{-1&1&1\\1&1&-1\\0&2&0}{0\\0\\0},

\amatrix{1&0&-1\\0&1&0\\0&0&0}{0\\0\\0}.

\begin{theorem}\label{thmtype:10.5.3}
Suppose the $n\times n$ matrix $A$ has an eigenvalue $\lambda_1$ of
multiplicity $\ge 3$ and the associated eigenspace is

$\lambda_1$ are linear combinations of two linearly independent
eigenvectors $\vect{X}_1$ and $\vect{X}_2$. Then there are constants
$\alpha$ and $\beta$ (not both zero) such that if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:10.5.12}
\vect{X}_3=\alpha\vect{X}_1+\beta\vect{X}_2,
\end{equation}
then there are infinitely many vectors $\vect{u}$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:10.5.13}
(A-\lambda_1I)\vect{u}=\vect{X}_3.
\end{equation}
If $\vect{u}$ satisfies  \eqref{eq:10.5.13}, then
\[\begin{aligned}
\vect{y}_1&=\vect{X}_1 e^{\lambda_1t},\\
\vect{y}_2&=\vect{X}_2e^{\lambda_1t},\qquad\text{and}\\
\vect{y}_3&=\vect{u}e^{\lambda_1t}+\vect{X}_3te^{\lambda_1t}
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:10.5.14},
\]
are linearly independent solutions of  $\vect{y}'=A\vect{y}.$
\end{theorem}

is a solution of  \eqref{eq:10.5.15}. Since $\vect{y}_1$, $\vect{y}_2$, and
$\vect{y}_3$ are linearly independent by \zcref{thmtype:10.5.3},
they form a fundamental set of solutions for \eqref{eq:10.5.15}.
Therefore the general solution of \eqref{eq:10.5.15} is
\[
\vect{y}=c_1\threecol101e^t+c_2\threecol010e^t
+c_3\left(\threecol{-1}00e^t+\threecol111te^t\right).\bbox
\]


\boxit{Geometric Properties of Solutions when  $n=2$}

We'll now  consider the geometric properties of solutions of a
$2\times2$ constant coefficient system
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.5.19}
\twocol{y_1'}{y_2'}=\begin{bmatrix}
a_{11}&a_{12}\\a_{21}&a_{22}
\end{bmatrix}\twocol{y_1}{y_2}
\end{equation}
under the assumptions of this section; that is, when the matrix
\[
A=\begin{bmatrix}a_{11}&a_{12}\\a_{21}&a_{22}\end{bmatrix}
\]
has a repeated eigenvalue $\lambda_1$ and the associated eigenspace is
one-dimensional. In this case we know from \zcref{thmtype:10.5.1}
that the general solution of \eqref{eq:10.5.19} is
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.5.20}
\vect{y}=c_1\vect{X}e^{\lambda_1t}+c_2(\vect{u}e^{\lambda_1t}+\vect{x}te^{\lambda_1t}),
\end{equation}
where $\vect{X}$ is an eigenvector of $A$ and $\vect{u}$ is any one of
the infinitely many solutions of
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.5.21}
(A-\lambda_1I)\vect{u}=\vect{X}.
\end{equation}
We assume that $\lambda_1\ne0$.

Let $L$ denote the line through the origin parallel to $\vect{X}$. By a
\emph{\color{blue}half-line} of $L$ we mean either of the rays obtained by
removing the origin from $L$. \zcref[S]{eq:10.5.20} is a parametric
equation of the half-line of $L$ in the direction of $\vect{X}$ if
$c_1>0$, or of the half-line of $L$ in the direction of $-\vect{X}$ if
$c_1<0$. The origin is the trajectory of the trivial solution $\vect{y}\equiv\vect{0}$.

Henceforth, we assume that $c_2\ne0$. In this case, the trajectory of
\eqref{eq:10.5.20} can't intersect $L$, since every point of $L$ is on a
trajectory obtained by setting $c_2=0$. Therefore the trajectory of
\eqref{eq:10.5.20} must lie entirely in one of the open half-planes
bounded
by $L$, but does not contain any point on $L$. Since the initial point
$(y_1(0),y_2(0))$ defined by $\vect{y}(0)=c_1\vect{X}_1+c_2\vect{u}$ is
on the trajectory, we can determine which half-plane contains the
trajectory from the sign of $c_2$, as shown in
\zcref{figure:10.5.1}.

the \emph{\color{blue}negative half-plane}. You should convince yourself
(\zcref{exer:10.5.35}) that even though there are infinitely
many vectors $\vect{u}$ that satisfy \eqref{eq:10.5.21}, they all define
the same positive and negative half-planes. In the figures simply
regard $\vect{u}$ as an arrow pointing to the positive half-plane,
since wen't attempted to give $\vect{u}$ its proper length or
direction in comparison with $\vect{X}$. For our purposes here, only the

whether the positive half-plane is to the right of an observer facing

Multiplying \eqref{eq:10.5.20} by $e^{-\lambda_1t}$ yields
\[
e^{-\lambda_1t}\vect{y}(t)=c_1\vect{X}+c_2\vect{u}+c_2t
\vect{X}.
\]
Since the last term on the right is dominant when $|t|$ is large,
this provides the following information on the direction of $\vect{y}(t)$:
\begin{alist}
\item % (a)
Along trajectories in the positive half-plane ($c_2>0$), the direction
of $\vect{y}(t)$ approaches the direction of $\vect{X}$ as $t\to\infty$
and the direction of $-\vect{X}$ as $t\to-\infty$.
\item % (b)
Along trajectories in the negative half-plane ($c_2<0$), the direction
of $\vect{y}(t)$ approaches the direction of $-\vect{X}$ as $t\to\infty$
and the direction of $\vect{X}$ as $t\to-\infty$.
\end{alist}

Since
\[
\lim_{t\to\infty}\|\vect{y}(t)\|=\infty\quad\text{and}\quad 
\lim_{t\to-\infty}\vect{y}(t)=\vect{0}\quad\text{if}\quad\lambda_1>0,
\]
or
\[
\lim_{t-\to\infty}\|\vect{y}(t)\|=\infty\quad\text{and}\quad 
\lim_{t\to\infty}\vect{y}(t)=\vect{0}\quad\text{if}\quad\lambda_1<0,
\]
 there are four possible patterns for the trajectories
of \eqref{eq:10.5.19}, depending upon the signs of $c_2$ and $\lambda_1$.
\zcref[range]{figure:10.5.2,figure:10.5.5} illustrate these patterns, and
reveal the following principle:

\emph{\color{blue}If $\lambda_1$ and $c_2$ have the same sign then the
direction of
the trajectory approaches the direction of $-\vect{X}$ as $\|\vect{y}
\|\to0$ and the direction of $\vect{X}$ as $\|\vect{y}\|\to\infty$. If
$\lambda_1$ and $c_2$ have opposite signs then the direction of the
trajectory approaches the direction of $\vect{X}$ as $\|\vect{y} \|\to0$
and the direction of $-\vect{X}$ as $\|\vect{y}\|\to\infty$.}

\section{Constant Coefficient Homogeneous Systems III}
\label{sec:system_const_coeff_iii}

We now consider the system $\vect{y}'=A\vect{y}$, where $A$ has a complex
eigenvalue $\lambda=\alpha+i\beta$ with $\beta\ne0$. We continue to
assume that $A$ has real entries, so the characteristic polynomial of
$A$ has real coefficients. This implies that
$\overline\lambda=\alpha-i\beta$ is also an eigenvalue of $A$.

An eigenvector $\vect{X}$ of $A$ associated with
$\lambda=\alpha+i\beta$ will have complex entries, so we'll write
\[
\vect{X}=\vect{u}+i\vect{v}
\]
where $\vect{u}$ and $\vect{v}$ have real entries; that is, $\vect{u}$
and $\vect{v}$ are the real and imaginary parts of $\vect{X}$. Since
$A\vect{X}=\lambda \vect{X}$,
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.6.1}
A(\vect{u}+i\vect{v})=(\alpha+i\beta)(\vect{u}+i\vect{v}).
\end{equation}
Taking complex conjugates here and recalling that $A$ has real entries
yields
\[
A(\vect{u}-i\vect{v})=(\alpha-i\beta)(\vect{u}-i\vect{v}),
\]
which shows that $\vect{X}=\vect{u}-i\vect{v}$ is an eigenvector
associated with $\overline\lambda=\alpha-i\beta$. The complex
conjugate eigenvalues $\lambda$ and $\overline\lambda$ can be
separately associated with linearly independent solutions $\vect{y}'=A\vect{y}$;     however, we won't pursue this approach, since solutions
obtained in this way turn out to be complex--valued. Instead, we'll
obtain solutions of $\vect{y}'=A\vect{y}$ in the form
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.6.2}
\vect{y}=f_1\vect{u}+f_2\vect{v}
\end{equation}
where $f_1$ and $f_2$ are real--valued scalar functions.
The next theorem shows how to do this.

\begin{theorem}\label{thmtype:10.6.1}
Let $A$ be an $n\times n$ matrix with real entries. Let
$\lambda=\alpha+i\beta$ ($\beta\ne0$) be a complex
eigenvalue of $A$ and let $\vect{X}=\vect{u}+i\vect{v}$ be an associated
eigenvector, where $\vect{u}$ and $\vect{v}$ have real components. Then
$\vect{u}$ and $\vect{v}$ are both nonzero and
\[
\vect{y}_1=e^{\alpha t}(\vect{u}\cos\beta t-\vect{v}\sin\beta t)
\quad\text{and}\quad
\vect{y}_2=e^{\alpha t}(\vect{u}\sin\beta t+\vect{v}\cos\beta t),
\]
which are the real and imaginary parts of
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.6.3}
e^{\alpha t}(\cos\beta t+i\sin\beta t)(\vect{u}+i\vect{v}),
\end{equation}
are linearly independent solutions of  $\vect{y}'=A\vect{y}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
A function of the form \eqref{eq:10.6.2} is a solution of $\vect{y}'=A\vect{y}$ if and only if
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.6.4}
f_1'\vect{u}+f_2'\vect{v}=f_1A\vect{u}+f_2A\vect{v}.
\end{equation}
Carrying out the multiplication indicated on the  right side
of \eqref{eq:10.6.1} and collecting the  real and imaginary parts of the
result yields
\[
A(\vect{u}+i\vect{v})=(\alpha\vect{u}-\beta\vect{v})+i(\alpha\vect{v}+\beta\vect{u}).
\]
Equating real and imaginary parts on the two sides of this equation yields
\begin{align*}
A\vect{u}&=\alpha\vect{u}-\beta\vect{v}\\
A\vect{v}&=\alpha\vect{v}+\beta\vect{u}.
\end{align*}
We leave it to you (\zcref{exer:10.6.25}) to show from this that
$\vect{u}$ and
$\vect{v}$ are both nonzero.
Substituting from these equations into \eqref{eq:10.6.4} yields
\begin{align*}
f_1'\vect{u}+f_2'\vect{v}
&=f_1(\alpha\vect{u}-\beta\vect{v})+f_2(\alpha\vect{v}+\beta\vect{u})\\
&=(\alpha f_1+\beta f_2)\vect{u}+(-\beta f_1+\alpha f_2)\vect{v}.
\end{align*}
This is true if
\[
\begin{aligned}
f_1'&=\alpha f_1+\beta f_2\phantom{,}\\
f_2'&=-\beta f_1+\alpha f_2,
\end{aligned}
\quad\text{or, equivalently,}\quad
\begin{aligned}
f_1'-\alpha f_1&=\phantom{-}\beta f_2\phantom{.}\\
f_2'-\alpha f_2&=-\beta f_1.
\end{aligned}
\]
If we let $f_1=g_1e^{\alpha t}$ and $f_2=g_2e^{\alpha t}$, where
$g_1$ and $g_2$ are to be determined, then the last two equations
become
\begin{align*}
g_1'&=\beta g_2\phantom{.}\\
g_2'&=-\beta g_1,
\end{align*}
which implies that
\[
g_1''=\beta g_2'=-\beta^2 g_1,
\]
so
\[
g_1''+\beta^2 g_1=0.
\]
The general solution of this equation is
\[
g_1=c_1\cos\beta t+c_2\sin\beta t.
\]
Moreover, since $g_2=g_1'/\beta$,
\[
g_2=-c_1\sin\beta t+c_2\cos\beta t.
\]
Multiplying  $g_1$  and $g_2$  by $e^{\alpha t}$ shows that
\begin{align*}
f_1&=e^{\alpha t}(\phantom{-}c_1\cos\beta t+c_2\sin\beta t ),\\
f_2&=e^{\alpha t}(-c_1\sin\beta t+c_2\cos\beta t).
\end{align*}
Substituting these into \eqref{eq:10.6.2} shows that
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.6.5}
\begin{aligned}
\vect{y}&=e^{\alpha t}\left[(c_1\cos\beta t+c_2\sin\beta t)\vect{u}
+(-c_1\sin\beta t+c_2\cos\beta t)\vect{v}\right]\\
&=c_1e^{\alpha t}(\vect{u}\cos\beta t-\vect{v}\sin\beta t)
+c_2e^{\alpha t}(\vect{u}\sin\beta t+\vect{v}\cos\beta t)
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
is a solution of $\vect{y}'=A\vect{y}$ for any choice of the constants
$c_1$ and $c_2$. In particular, by first taking $c_1=1$ and $c_2=0$
and then taking $c_1=0$ and $c_2=1$, we see that $\vect{y}_1$ and $\vect{y}_2$ are solutions of $ \vect{y}'=A\vect{y}$. We leave it to you to
verify that they are, respectively, the real and imaginary parts of
\eqref{eq:10.6.3} (\zcref{exer:10.6.26}), and that they are linearly
independent (\zcref{exer:10.6.27}).
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{example:10.6.1}
 Find the general solution of
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.6.6}
\vect{y}'=\twobytwo4{-5}5{-2}\vect{y}.
\end{equation}
\end{example}

\solution The characteristic polynomial of the coefficient matrix $A$
in \eqref{eq:10.6.6} is
\[
\begin{vmatrix}4-\lambda&-5\\ 5&-2-\lambda\end{vmatrix}=(\lambda-1)^2+16.
\]
Hence, $\lambda=1+4i$ is an eigenvalue of $A$. The associated
eigenvectors satisfy $\left(A-\left(1+4i\right)I\right)\vect{X}=\vect{0}$. The augmented matrix of this system is
\[
\amatrix{3-4i&-5\\5&-3-4i}{0\\0},
 \]
which is row equivalent to
\[
\amatrix{1&-\frac{3+4i}{5}\\0&0}{0\\0}.
\]
Therefore $x_1=(3+4i)x_2/5$. Taking $x_2=5$ yields $x_1=3+4i$, so
\[
\vect{X}=\begin{bmatrix}3+4i\\5\end{bmatrix}
\]
is an eigenvector.  The real and imaginary parts of
\[
e^t(\cos4t+i\sin4t)\begin{bmatrix}3+4i\\5\end{bmatrix}
\]
 are
\[
\vect{y}_1=e^t\begin{bmatrix}3\cos4t-4\sin4t\\5\cos4t\end{bmatrix}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\vect{y}_2=e^t\begin{bmatrix}3\sin4t+4\cos4t\\5\sin4t\end{bmatrix},
\]
which are linearly independent solutions of  \eqref{eq:10.6.6}.
The general solution of \eqref{eq:10.6.6} is
\[
\vect{y}=
c_1e^t\begin{bmatrix}3\cos4t-4\sin4t\\5\cos4t\end{bmatrix}+
c_2e^t\begin{bmatrix}3\sin4t+4\cos4t\\5\sin4t\end{bmatrix}.
\]

Hence, $\lambda=1+3i$ is an eigenvalue of $A$. The associated

 \]
which is row equivalent to
\[
\amatrix{1&\frac{-5+i}{2}\\0&0}{0\\0}.
\]
Therefore $x_1=(5-i)/2$. Taking $x_2=2$ yields $x_1=5-i$, so
\[
\vect{X}=\begin{bmatrix}5-i\\2\end{bmatrix}
\]
is an eigenvector. The real and imaginary parts of

 are

\vect{y}_1=e^t\begin{bmatrix}\sin3t+5\cos3t\\2\cos3t\end{bmatrix}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\vect{y}_2=e^t\begin{bmatrix}-\cos3t+5\sin3t\\2\sin3t\end{bmatrix},
 \]
which are  linearly independent solutions of  \eqref{eq:10.6.7}.

\boxit{Geometric Properties of Solutions when  $n=2$}

We'll now  consider the geometric properties of solutions of a
$2\times2$ constant coefficient system
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.6.10}
\twocol{y_1'}{y_2'}=\begin{bmatrix}a_{11}&a_{12}\\a_{21}&a_{22}\end{bmatrix}
\twocol{y_1}{y_2}
\end{equation}
under the assumptions of this section; that is, when the matrix
\[
A=\begin{bmatrix}a_{11}&a_{12}\\a_{21}&a_{22}\end{bmatrix}
\]
has a complex eigenvalue $\lambda=\alpha+i\beta$
% TODO is this the first occurrence of (u,v) for inner product?
($\beta\ne0$) and $\vect{X}=\vect{u}+i\vect{v}$ is an
associated eigenvector, where $\vect{u}$ and $\vect{v}$ have real
components. To describe the trajectories accurately it's necessary to
introduce a new rectangular coordinate system in the $y_1$-$y_2$
plane. This raises a point that hasn't come up before: It is always
possible to choose $\vect{X}$ so that $(\vect{u},\vect{v})=0$. A special
effort is required to do this, since not every eigenvector has this
property. However, if we know an eigenvector that doesn't, we can
multiply it by a suitable complex constant to obtain one that does. To
see this, note that if $\vect{X}$ is a $\lambda$-eigenvector of $A$ and
$k$ is an arbitrary real number, then
\[
\vect{X}_1=(1+ik)\vect{X}=(1+ik)(\vect{u}+i\vect{v})
=(\vect{u}-k\vect{v})+i(\vect{v}+k\vect{u})
\]
is also a $\lambda$-eigenvector of $A$, since
\[
A\vect{X}_1= A((1+ik)\vect{X})=(1+ik)A\vect{X}=(1+ik)\lambda\vect{X}=
\lambda((1+ik)\vect{X})=\lambda\vect{X}_1.
\]
The real and imaginary parts of $\vect{X}_1$ are
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.6.11}
\vect{u}_1=\vect{u}-k\vect{v} \quad\text{and}\quad 
\vect{v}_1=\vect{v}+k\vect{u},
\end{equation}
so
\[
(\vect{u}_1,\vect{v}_1)=(\vect{u}-k\vect{v},\vect{v}+k\vect{u})
=-\left[(\vect{u},\vect{v})k^2+(\|\vect{v}\|^2-\|\vect{u}\|^2)k
-(\vect{u},\vect{v})\right].
\]
Therefore $(\vect{u}_1,\vect{v}_1)=0$ if
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.6.12}
(\vect{u},\vect{v})k^2+(\|\vect{v}\|^2-\|\vect{u}\|^2)k-(\vect{u},\vect{v})=0.
\end{equation}
If $(\vect{u},\vect{v})\ne0$ we can use the quadratic formula to find
two real values of $k$ such that $(\vect{u}_1,\vect{v}_1)=0$
(\zcref{exer:10.6.28}).

\begin{example}\label{example:10.6.5}
In \zcref{example:10.6.1}
we found the eigenvector
\[
\vect{X}=\ctwocol{3+4i}5=\twocol35+i\twocol40
\]
for the matrix of the system \eqref{eq:10.6.6}. Here $\vect{u}=\twocol35$
and $\vect{v}=\twocol40$ are not orthogonal, since $(\vect{u},\vect{v})=12$. Since $\|\vect{v}\|^2-\|\vect{u}\|^2=-18$, \eqref{eq:10.6.12} is
equivalent to
\[
2k^2-3k-2=0.
\]
The zeros of this equation are $k_1=2$ and $k_2=-1/2$. Letting $k=2$
in \eqref{eq:10.6.11} yields
\[
\vect{u}_1=\vect{u}-2\vect{v}=\twocol{-5}{\phantom{-}5}\quad\text{and}\quad 
\vect{v}_1=\vect{v}+2\vect{u}=\twocol{10}{10},
\]
and $(\vect{u}_1,\vect{v}_1)=0$.
 Letting $k=-1/2$
in \eqref{eq:10.6.11} yields
\[
\vect{u}_1=\vect{u}+\frac{\vect{v}}{2}=\twocol{5}5\quad\text{and}\quad 
\vect{v}_1=\vect{v}-\frac{\vect{u}}{2}=\frac{1}{2}\twocol{-5}{\phantom{-}5},
\]
and again $(\vect{u}_1,\vect{v}_1)=0$. \bbox
\end{example}

You'll need a calculator or computer to do

Henceforth, we'll assume that $(\vect{u},\vect{v})=0$. Let $\vect{u}$ and
$\vect{v}$ be unit vectors in the directions of $\vect{u}$ and $\vect{v}$, respectively; that is, $\vect{u}=\vect{u}/\|\vect{u}\|$ and $\vect{V}=\vect{v}/\|\vect{v}\|$. The new rectangular coordinate system will
have the same origin as the $y_1$-$y_2$ system. The coordinates of a
point in this system will be denoted by $(z_1,z_2)$, where $z_1$ and
$z_2$ are the displacements in the directions of $\vect{u}$ and $\vect{V}$, respectively.

From \eqref{eq:10.6.5}, the solutions of \eqref{eq:10.6.10} are given by
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.6.13} \vect{y}=e^{\alpha t}\left[(c_1\cos\beta
t+c_2\sin\beta t)\vect{u} +(-c_1\sin\beta t+c_2\cos\beta t)\vect{v}\right]. \end{equation} For convenience, let's call the curve
traversed by $e^{-\alpha t}\vect{y}(t)$ a \emph{\color{blue}shadow trajectory} of
\eqref{eq:10.6.10}. Multiplying \eqref{eq:10.6.13} by $e^{-\alpha t}$ yields
\[
e^{-\alpha t}\vect{y}(t)=z_1(t)\vect{u}+z_2(t)\vect{v},
\]
where
\begin{align*}
z_1(t)&=\|\vect{u}\|(c_1\cos\beta t+c_2\sin\beta t)\\
z_2(t)&=\|\vect{v}\|(-c_1\sin\beta t+c_2\cos\beta t).
\end{align*}
Therefore
\[ \frac{(z_1(t))^2}{\|\vect{u}\|^2}+\frac{(z_2(t))^2}{\|\vect{v}\|^2} =c_1^2+c_2^2
\]
(verify!), which means that the shadow trajectories of \eqref{eq:10.6.10}
are ellipses centered at the origin, with axes of symmetry parallel to
$\vect{u}$ and $\vect{v}$. Since
\[
z_1'=\frac{\beta\|\vect{u}\|}{\|\vect{v}\|} z_2\quad\text{and}\quad 
z_2'=-\frac{\beta\|\vect{v}\|}{\|\vect{u}\|} z_1,
\]

origin to a point on the shadow ellipse rotates in the same direction
that $\vect{v}$ would have to be rotated by $\pi/2$ radians to bring it
into coincidence with $\vect{u}$ (\zcref{figure:10.6.1,figure:10.6.2}).

\section{Variation of Parameters for Nonhomogeneous Linear Systems}
\label{sec:systems_variation_param}

We now consider the nonhomogeneous linear system
\[
\vect{y}'= A(t)\vect{y}+\vect{F}(t),
\]
where $A$ is an $n\times n$ matrix function and $\vect{F}$ is an
$n$-vector forcing function. Associated with this system is the
\emph{\color{blue}complementary system} $\vect{y}'=A(t)\vect{y}$.

The next theorem is analogous to
\zcref{thmtype:5.3.2,thmtype:9.1.5}. It shows how to find the general solution
of
$\vect{y}'=A(t)\vect{y}+\vect{F}(t)$ if we know a particular solution of $\vect{y}'=A(t)\vect{y}+\vect{F}(t)$ and a fundamental set of solutions of the
complementary system. We leave the proof as an exercise
(\zcref{exer:10.7.21}).

\begin{theorem}\color{blue}\label{thmtype:10.7.1}
Suppose the $n\times n$ matrix function $A$ and the $n$-vector
function $\vect{F}$ are continuous on $(a,b).$ Let $\vect{y}_p$ be a
particular solution of $\vect{y}'=A(t)\vect{y}+\vect{F}(t)$ on $(a,b)$,
and let
$\{\vect{y}_1,\vect{y}_2,\dots,\vect{y}_n\}$ be a fundamental set of
solutions of the complementary equation $\vect{y}'=A(t)\vect{y}$ on
$(a,b)$. Then $\vect{y}$ is a solution of $\vect{y}'=A(t)\vect{y}+\vect{f}(t)$ on $(a,b)$ if and only if
\[
\vect{y}=\vect{y}_p+c_1\vect{y}_1+c_2\vect{y}_2+\cdots+c_n\vect{y}_n,
\]
where $c_1,$ $c_2,$ \dots, $c_n$  are constants.
\end{theorem}


\boxit{Finding a Particular Solution of a Nonhomogeneous System}

We now discuss an extension of the method of variation of parameters
to linear nonhomogeneous systems. This method will produce a
particular solution of a nonhomogeneous system $\vect{y}'=A(t)\vect{y}+\vect{F}(t)$ provided that we know a fundamental matrix for the
complementary system. To derive the method, suppose $Y$ is a
fundamental matrix for the complementary system; that is,
\[
Y=\begin{bmatrix}
y_{11}&y_{12}&\cdots&y_{1n} \\
y_{21}&y_{22}&\cdots&y_{2n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots \\
y_{n1}&y_{n2}&\cdots&y_{nn} \\
\end{bmatrix},
\]
where
\[
\vect{y}_1=\begin{bmatrix}y_{11}\\y_{21}\\ \vdots\\y_{n1}\end{bmatrix},\quad
\vect{y}_2=\begin{bmatrix}y_{12}\\y_{22}\\ \vdots\\y_{n2}\end{bmatrix},
\quad \cdots,\quad
\vect{y}_n=\begin{bmatrix}y_{1n}\\y_{2n}\\ \vdots\\y_{nn}\end{bmatrix}
\]
is a fundamental set of solutions of the complementary system.
In \zcref{sec:system_theory} we saw that $Y'=A(t)Y$. We seek a
particular solution of
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.7.1}
\vect{y}'=A(t)\vect{y}+\vect{F}(t)
\end{equation}
 of the form
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.7.2}
\vect{y}_p=Y\vect{u},
\end{equation}
 where $\vect{u}$ is to be determined. Differentiating \eqref{eq:10.7.2} yields
\begin{align*}
 \vect{y}_p'&=Y' \vect{u}+Y \vect{u}'\\
 &=A Y \vect{u}+Y \vect{u}'\text{ (since $Y'=AY$)}\\
 &= A\vect{y}_p+Y \vect{u}'\text{ (since $Y\vect{u}=\vect{y}_p$)}.
\end{align*}
Comparing this with  \eqref{eq:10.7.1} shows that $\vect{y}_p=Y\vect{u}$ is a
solution of \eqref{eq:10.7.1} if and only if
\[
Y\vect{u}'=\vect{F}.
\]
Thus, we can find a particular solution $\vect{y}_p$ by solving this
equation for $\vect{u}'$, integrating to obtain $\vect{u}$, and
computing $Y\vect{u}$. We can take all constants of integration to be
zero, since any particular solution will suffice.

\zcref{exer:10.7.22} sketches a proof that this method is analogous
to the method of variation of parameters discussed in
\zcref{sec:variation_param,{sec:higher_variation_param}} for scalar linear
equations.

\begin{example}\label{example:10.7.1}
\begin{alist}
\item % (a)
Find a particular solution of the system
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.7.3}
\vect{y}'=\twobytwo1221\vect{y}+\begin{bmatrix}2e^{4t}\\e^{4t}\end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
which we considered in \zcref{example:10.2.1}.
\item % (b)
Find the general solution of  \eqref{eq:10.7.3}.
\end{alist}
\end{example}

\begin{alistSolution}
\item The complementary system is
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.7.4}
\vect{y}'=\twobytwo1221\vect{y}.
\end{equation}
The characteristic polynomial of the coefficient matrix is
\[
\begin{vmatrix}1-\lambda&2\\2&1-\lambda\end{vmatrix}=
(\lambda+1)(\lambda-3).
\]
Using the method of \zcref{sec:system_const_coeff_i}, we find that
\[
\vect{y}_1=\begin{bmatrix}e^{3t}\\e^{3t}\end{bmatrix}
\quad\text{and}\quad 
\vect{y}_2=\begin{bmatrix}e^{-t}\\-e^{-t}\end{bmatrix}
\]
are linearly independent solutions of \eqref{eq:10.7.4}.
Therefore
\[
Y=\begin{bmatrix}e^{3t}&e^{-t}\\e^{3t}&-e^{-t}\end{bmatrix}
\]
is a fundamental matrix for \eqref{eq:10.7.4}. We seek a particular
solution $\vect{y}_p=Y\vect{u}$ of \eqref{eq:10.7.3}, where $Y\vect{u}'=\vect{f}$; that is,
\[
\begin{bmatrix}e^{3t}&e^{-t}\\e^{3t}&-e^{-t}\end{bmatrix}
\twocol{u_1'}{u_2'}
=\begin{bmatrix}2e^{4t}\\e^{4t}\end{bmatrix}.
\]
The determinant of $Y$ is the Wronskian
\[
\begin{vmatrix}e^{3t}&e^{-t}\\e^{3t}&-e^{-t}\end{vmatrix}
=-2e^{2t}.
\]
By Cramer's rule,
\begin{alignat*}{2}
u_1'&=-\dfrac{1}{2e^{2t}}
\begin{vmatrix}2e^{4t}&e^{-t}\\e^{4t}&-e^{-t}\end{vmatrix}
&{}=\dfrac{3e^{3t}}{2e^{2t}}
&=\dfrac{3}{2}e^t,\\
u_2'&=-\dfrac{1}{2e^{2t}}
\begin{vmatrix}e^{3t}&2e^{4t}\\e^{3t}&e^{4t}\end{vmatrix}
&=\dfrac{e^{7t}}{2e^{2t}}
&=\dfrac{1}{2}e^{5t}.
\end{alignat*}
 Therefore
\[
\vect{u}'=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}3e^t\\e^{5t}\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Integrating and taking the constants of integration to be zero yields
\[
\vect{u}=\frac{1}{10}\begin{bmatrix}15e^t\\e^{5t}\end{bmatrix},
\]
so
\[
\vect{y}_p=Y\vect{u}=
\dfrac{1}{10}\begin{bmatrix}e^{3t}&e^{-t}\\e^{3t}&-e^{-t}\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}15e^t\\e^{5t}\end{bmatrix}
=\phantom{0}
\dfrac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix}8e^{4t}\\7e^{4t}\end{bmatrix}
\]
is a particular solution of  \eqref{eq:10.7.3}.

\item From \zcref{thmtype:10.7.1}, the general solution of
\eqref{eq:10.7.3} is
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.7.5}
\vect{y}=\vect{y}_p+c_1\vect{y}_1+c_2\vect{y}_2=
\frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix}8e^{4t}\\7e^{4t}\end{bmatrix}
+c_1\begin{bmatrix}e^{3t}\\e^{3t}\end{bmatrix}
+c_2\begin{bmatrix}e^{-t}\\-e^{-t}\end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}
which can also be written as
\[
\vect{y}=
\frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix}8e^{4t}\\7e^{4t}\end{bmatrix}
+\begin{bmatrix}e^{3t}&e^{-t}\\e^{3t}&-e^{-t}\end{bmatrix}
\vect{c},
\]
where $\vect{c}$ is an arbitrary constant vector.

Writing \eqref{eq:10.7.5} in terms of
coordinates yields
\begin{align*}
y_1&=\frac{8}{5}e^{4t}+c_1e^{3t}+c_2e^{-t}\\
y_2&=\frac{7}{5}e^{4t}+c_1e^{3t}-c_2e^{-t},
\end{align*}
so our result is consistent with \zcref{example:10.2.1}.
\bbox.
\end{alistSolution}

If $A$ isn't  a constant matrix, it's usually difficult to find a
fundamental set of solutions for the system $\vect{y}'=A(t)\vect{y}$. It
is beyond the scope of this text to discuss methods for doing this.
Therefore, in the following examples and in the exercises involving
systems with variable coefficient matrices we'll provide fundamental
matrices for the complementary systems without explaining how they
were obtained.

\begin{example}\label{example:10.7.2}
Find a particular solution of
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10.7.6}
\vect{y}'=\begin{bmatrix}2&2e^{-2t}\\2e^{2t}&4\end{bmatrix}
\vect{y}+\twocol11,
\end{equation}
given that
\[
Y=\begin{bmatrix}e^{4t}&-1\\e^{6t}&e^{2t}\end{bmatrix}
\]
is  a fundamental matrix for the complementary system.
\end{example}

\solution
We seek a particular solution
$\vect{y}_p=Y\vect{u}$ of \eqref{eq:10.7.6} where
 $Y\vect{u}'=\vect{F}$; that is,
\[
\begin{bmatrix}e^{4t}&-1\\e^{6t}&e^{2t}\end{bmatrix}
\twocol{u_1'}{u_2'}=\twocol11.
\]
The determinant of $Y$ is the Wronskian
\[
\begin{vmatrix}e^{4t}&-1\\e^{6t}&e^{2t}\end{vmatrix}=2e^{6t}.
\]
By Cramer's rule,
\begin{alignat*}{3}
u_1'&=\dfrac{1}{2e^{6t}}
\begin{vmatrix}1&-1\\1&e^{2t}\end{vmatrix}
&&=\dfrac{e^{2t}+1}{2e^{6t}}
&&=\dfrac{e^{-4t}+e^{-6t}}{2}\\
u_2'&=\dfrac{1}{2e^{6t}}
\begin{vmatrix}e^{4t}&1\\e^{6t}&1\end{vmatrix}
&&=\dfrac{e^{4t}-e^{6t}}{2e^{6t}}
&&=\dfrac{e^{-2t}-1}{2}.
\end{alignat*}
Therefore
\[
\vect{u}'=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}e^{-4t}+e^{-6t}\\e^{-2t}-1\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Integrating  and taking the constants of integration to be zero yields
\[
\vect{u}=-\frac{1}{24}\begin{bmatrix}3e^{-4t}+2e^{-6t}\\6e^{-2t}+12t\end{bmatrix},
\]
so
\[
\vect{y}_p=Y\vect{u}=
-\frac{1}{24}\begin{bmatrix}e^{4t}&-1\\e^{6t}&e^{2t}\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}3e^{-4t}+2e^{-6t}\\6e^{-2t}+12t\end{bmatrix}
=\frac{1}{24}\begin{bmatrix}4e^{-2t}+12t-3\\-3e^{2t}(4t+1)-8\end{bmatrix}
\]
is a particular solution of  \eqref{eq:10.7.6}.

Find a particular solution of

\solution
We seek a particular solution $\vect{y}_p=Y\vect{u}$ of \eqref{eq:10.7.7}
where $Y\vect{u}'=\vect{F}$; that is,
\[
\begin{bmatrix}2t&3t^2\\1&2t\end{bmatrix}\twocol{u_1'}{u_2'}
=\twocol{t^2}{t^2}.
\]
The determinant of $Y$  is the Wronskian
\[
\begin{vmatrix}2t&3t^2\\1&2t\end{vmatrix}=t^2.
\]
By Cramer's rule,
\begin{alignat*}{3}
u_1'
&=\dfrac{1}{t^2}
\begin{vmatrix}t^2&3t^2\\t^2&2t\end{vmatrix}
&&=\dfrac{2t^3-3t^4}{t^2}
&&=2t-3t^2,

u_2'
&=\dfrac{1}{t^2}
\begin{vmatrix}2t&t^2\\1&t^2\end{vmatrix}
&&=\dfrac{2t^3-t^2}{t^2}
&&=2t-1.
\end{alignat*}
Therefore
\[
\vect{u}'=\begin{bmatrix}2t-3t^2\\2t-1\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Integrating  and taking the constants of integration to be zero yields
\[
\vect{u}=\begin{bmatrix}t^2-t^3\\t^2-t\end{bmatrix},
\]
so
\[
\vect{y}_p=Y\vect{u}=
\begin{bmatrix}2t&3t^2\\1&2t\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}t^2-t^3\\t^2-t\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}t^3(t-1)\\t^2(t-1)\end{bmatrix}

\begin{bmatrix}e^t&0&e^{2t}\\0&e^{3t}&e^{3t}\\e^{-t}&1&0\end{bmatrix}

u_1'
&=-\dfrac{1}{2e^{4t}}
\begin{vmatrix}1&0&e^{2t}\\e^t&e^{3t}&e^{3t}\\e^{-t}&1&0\end{vmatrix}
&&=\dfrac{e^{4t}}{2e^{4t}}
&&=\dfrac{1}{2}\\
u_2'
&=-\dfrac{1}{2e^{4t}}
\begin{vmatrix}e^t&1&e^{2t}\\0&e^t&e^{3t}\\e^{-t}&e^{-t}&0\end{vmatrix}
&&=\dfrac{e^{3t}}{2e^{4t}}
&&=\dfrac{1}{2}e^{-t}\\
u_3'
&=-\dfrac{1}{2e^{4t}}
\begin{vmatrix}e^t&0&1\\0&e^{3t}&e^t\\e^{-t}&1&e^{-t}\end{vmatrix}
&&=-\dfrac{e^{3t}-2e^{2t}}{2e^{4t}}
&&=\dfrac{2e^{-2t}-e^{-t}}{2}.
\end{alignat*}
Therefore
\[
\vect{u}'=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}1\\e^{-t}\\2e^{-2t}-e^{-t}\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Integrating  and taking the constants of integration to be zero yields
\[
\vect{u}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}t\\-e^{-t}\\e^{-t}-e^{-2t}\end{bmatrix},
\]
so
\[
\vect{y}_p=Y\vect{u}=
\frac{1}{2}
\begin{bmatrix}e^t&0&e^{2t}\\0&e^{3t}&e^{3t}\\e^{-t}&1&0\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}t\\-e^{-t}\\e^{-t}-e^{-2t}\end{bmatrix}
=\dfrac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}e^t(t+1)-1\\-e^t\\e^{-t}(t-1)\end{bmatrix}
\]
is a particular solution of  \eqref{eq:10.7.10}.
